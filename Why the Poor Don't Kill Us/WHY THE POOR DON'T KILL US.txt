About the Book

In this searing and darkly hilarious diagnosis of contemporary society, acclaimed Indian writer Manu Joseph explores why the poor don’t rise in revolt against the rich despite living in one of the most unequal regions of the world.

The poor know how much we spend in a single day, on a single meal, the price of Atlantic salmon and avocados. “Why,” he asks, “do they tolerate it? Why don’t they crawl out from their catastrophes and finish us off? Why don’t little men emerge from manholes and attack the cars? Why don’t the maids, who squat like frogs beside kitchen sinks, pull out the hair of their conscientious madams who never give them a day off? Why is there peace?”

Why the Poor Don’t Kill Us lays bare, with pitiless precision, the absurd, obvious and counter-intuitive reasons why we are safe. So far. It is a fragile peace, and we need to understand how it has come to be.

CHAPTER 1: WHO IS POOR?
-----------------------

ON the morning of 26 January 2001, a massive earthquake struck western India. Twenty hours later, I was in Bhuj, in Gujarat, very close to the epicentre. Among the first people I met was a man who stood on top of the debris of his home. His wife and children were trapped in the rubble. The army was yet to arrive and there was no rescue operation underway. He said he could hear his family occasionally call for help. They would fall silent for a long period, then he would hear them again. His home had sunk into the foundation, so his family was probably deeper inside than it appeared from ground level. I did not know how to speak to a man in such a situation. I was twenty-five. I thought I would leave him alone. I turned to a bystander and asked for directions to a public ground, where I had heard there was a medical camp. But the man whose family was buried beneath his feet intervened and began to give me directions in a very elaborate way, his fingers telling me where I should turn left and where I should turn right. I am certain there are theories to explain his behaviour, but we will never know what was going on in his mind. When I left him, he just stood there, like a man waiting for a bus.

Over the next few days, the photographer I accompanied, Prashant Panjiar, was approached several times by people, chiefly men, who asked him to take pictures of their dead, chiefly women. We were at first perplexed by this behaviour but soon realized that they were asking him to take the pictures as proof of death because in a natural calamity like this, a press photograph was as good as a death certificate. Men whose wives were photographed dead could then remarry. Adult children of parents who were photographed dead could settle property matters. How do people get to know such municipal bylaws hours after they have lost their own? One time, a man at a funeral waved at us, and requested us to shoot him against the backdrop of the burning pyre of his dead brother. (There were so many bodies that people were allowed to burn their dead anywhere.) It was the man’s bad luck that he couldn’t find us before he had consigned his brother to the flames. He was in his brother’s will, he said, and he needed photographic evidence of the funeral pyre. How could the image of a funeral pyre help him establish a specific death? Apparently he had a point. It would work, in a situation like this.

Later, in Anjar, where more than 400 children had perished in their school, I saw a man sitting on top of a cupboard that stood tilted on rubble. In that rubble was the body of his sixteen-year-old brother. He was breaking the cupboard with a knife. He told me there were jewels, money, and perfume bottles inside. He wanted to extract them before the army got to them. Later, a soldier would tell me, ‘Once they realize their family is dead, they quickly think of what to do next.’ He was disturbed; he said he had not seen this sort of mass behaviour before.

In Gandhidham, a town that was badly affected, there were vast areas where the smell of rotting bodies filled the air. Stray dogs had become very aggressive; they were probably eating the dead who were stuck in the debris. Standing in that stench, the people of Gandhidham told me how the media was unfairly portraying Bhuj as the calamity zone, sending all monetary relief to that town. I came across a rescue operation. A middle-aged man was deep in the debris; his leg stuck under a slab of stone; a decaying corpse beside him, which he did not know was his wife’s. He begged the soldiers to chop off his leg and get him out. A soldier told me, ‘Just now his relative took me aside and asked me to kill him. That way we can save him from all the misery. Give him an injection or something, that man told me.’

There was more recognizable grief all around me; people did behave in very familiar ways, agreeable ways. Men and women stood weeping. When couples cry together, it is the darkest thing. Someone told me, though I don’t know still if he was talking about himself or just making a literal exclamation, ‘How can you cover your nose with a kerchief and hide from what is the only remnant of your family?’

Four days after the quake, a newborn was pulled out from the debris, as alive as he was the day he was born. He was found in the embrace of his mother, who was dead. She had probably died soon after her home had collapsed on her, and may have shielded the baby in a final act. As the rescued baby was held in the arms of a person, a crowd of neighbours and strangers encircled the child. One by one, they touched the baby. I too inched forward for a chance to touch him. But we had also quickly grown used to the dead. At least once I saw a dead woman in a ditch, with a large stone placed on her helpfully by someone for people to step on her and cross over. People carried the dead in vegetable carts. A mighty young man pulled down a rope that ran across a beam, and with every heave, from the debris rose a suspended old woman, his dead mother. Now and then, he left her dangling, to rest, or to discuss with someone how to get her down, as she gently swung.

In the days following the quake, as the full scale of human nature unfolded, all classes exhibited a degree of practicality. It was as though the whole region, both the poor and the middle classes and the rich, had suddenly collapsed into antiquity. It was not entirely unfamiliar to me as an Indian. I have seen this feral pragmatism before. They were behaving like poor people.

What had in fact happened to them was that they had become raw, shorn of the thin skin of culture and sensitivity that had accumulated over humans over centuries. They had simply moved back in time.

The poor do not need a natural catastrophe to be that way. They are ancient people living in our modern times.

Strange things happen to them. Dozens die because they had some cheap medicine for the common cold; mysterious nameless fevers rage only among them. Now and then a little man who went down a manhole to manually remove shit, is ‘asphyxiated’. Children die painfully of rabies. Boys on a 10-kilometre run to qualify as police constables drop dead because they were made to run in extreme heat and humidity. Deep inside Earth, ‘rat miners’ crawl into crevices to collect coal because robots are more expensive than them. A pregnant maid has a miscarriage; she appears the next morning for work. The jails are filled with the young who have never been proven guilty yet have been rotting away for years.

Strange good things, too, happen. They ingest weakened polio virus because their water is contaminated by faeces of those who have had oral polio drops, and the poorest are thus inadvertently vaccinated.1

We live in times where everyone has some claim to poverty. Including intellectuals who had started out as affluent, who have now been priced out of their old privileges by the new rich. They cannot afford five-star resorts any more; they have to go to ‘boutique’ hotels. There are people in the middle classes, though, whose poverty is a bit more poignant. It is true that many of the readers of this book, though middle class, are just a cancer away from poverty. And there are millions in Mumbai who live in one-room homes with half a dozen people, in humid tenements called chawls. Yet, even they are not the poor by Indian standards. In fact, they form the new middle class of India; some of whom today own cars that are almost as big as their homes, and their one-room homes are air-conditioned. Even the farmers on tractors during the farmers’ protests against the three farm bills, the guys whom singer Rihanna thought were poor and exclaimed on Twitter, ‘Why isn’t anyone talking about it?’, are actually the agrarian middle class, and the farmers’ movement itself was a North Indian middle-class movement, which of course employed or drew many poor farm workers to join the protests.

I won’t deny anyone their poverty. In fact, in this book, I use the word ‘poor’ to mean anyone who does not feel economically comfortable in a particular situation, and all the categories in the preceding paragraph will qualify. But I chiefly mean the very poor, the sort of people whom India considers poor, people who are centuries behind.

When we speak of ancient people, we think they were very different sort of humans from us. But they were not that different; they felt everything we feel. I even believe, though I have no evidence, that men deeply loved women, fathers deeply loved their daughters, some of whom may have even preferred to eat leaves instead of meat. But the ancients differed in one quality. They never thought they were in control; they conceded that misery was inevitable and that man was helpless. They could be destroyed easily by the rich, and the die was so cast against them that it appeared God himself willed this. That is exactly how the modern poor are. In 2024, a Tamil spiritual speaker told an audience of impoverished people that they were so because of their past sins. (The audience appeared to accept it, except one heroic man who objected.) The modern poor are worse than the ancients because to be ancient in a modern era is doubly cruel.

The mystery is, when they can see the riches around them, the other eras in which luckier people live, why do they tolerate it? Why don’t they crawl out from their catastrophes and finish us off? Why don’t little men emerge from manholes and attack the cars? Why don’t the maids who squat like frogs by kitchen sinks pull out the hair of their conscientious madams who never give them a day off or pay them as much as they pay at least the drivers? Why do the poor tolerate the rich?

Why is there peace? Why do we get away? Why don’t the poor kill us?

IN July 2017, a mob of maids and their men attacked a cluster of apartment blocks in Noida, a vast middle- class towns in the National Capital Region. They did this after a young maid went missing and her folks thought she had been detained by a family that had earlier accused her of stealing money. A few months later, elsewhere in Noida, a resident who abused the guards for accidentally turning off the Wi-Fi was beaten up by them. At the time, these events were news because they were unusual. Such scuffles are more frequent now across India, but they are still news; they are not as common as they should be.

The Indian servant is not as submissive as they were when I was a child. Guards still salute, but that old reverence with which they used to salute our fathers, there is none. Maids are even talking back; who knows one day they may even demand long- handled mops to clean the floors. The rich wonder if it is all because of the political appeasement of the poor, and the employment guarantee schemes conceived by profligate governments. The poor seem to have options these days. The wages, too, have risen. Carpenters and plumbers are turning up alone without an impoverished assistant because it is not as cheap as before to hire help in India.

Meanwhile, the middle class, at least the young and the refined, want to be nice to their servants, whom they do not call ‘servants’ any more, but they don’t want the job abolished, of course. They just want to be nicer than their parents. The new conscientious sahib even exclaims on social media how cruel it is that maids are ‘expected to be invisible’. The madam, though as conscientious, is unlikely to say that, or let the maid use the toilet, or the cutlery. Most Indian homes, including those of writers with a moral compass, have separate cutlery for servants. However, some maids these days are sitting on footstools, even chairs, in the presence of their employers. If there is a nice time-lapse video of seated maids across a stretch of the past twenty years, it would show them slowly soaring from floor to sofa. But it is still common to see them sit on the floor and eat because the homes expect that, or the servants themselves feel they don’t belong on the sofas. The poor, taken together, are still meek. Considering their lives and the lives of the rich that they see every day, they appear to be far less furious than they should be.

But who exactly are these poor who have a reason to be violent? I do not have any poetic grouse against statistics; in fact, I find the numbers they can contain very moving. But now and then, an incident occurs that answers the question of India’s poverty in a way that also conveys the full meaning of poverty.

There is a photograph of a man carrying what at first glance appears to be a heavy artefact. He is walking with his daughter who is almost a teenager. He was seen in August 2016. His name is Dana Majhi, and what he was carrying was his dead wife. At the time when the photograph was taken by curious passers- by, he had walked over 10 kilometres carrying her corpse. She had died a few hours ago in a government hospital, of tuberculosis. The hospital asked Dana to take her away but he did not have the money to hire a vehicle to transfer her body to their village on top of a hill, about 60 kilometres away. According to Dana, the hospital refused to help him with a vehicle. So he wrapped Amang, his wife, in cloth and set out on foot with his daughter. On occasion he placed her body on the ground to rest, and on occasion, the girl wept for her mother, who was only forty-two.

Did Dana, too, weep? Did he love Amang? Did he ever carry her in happier times? Did they have happier times? A man carrying his woman on his shoulder appears to be performing an act of love, even if she is dead. Or maybe it was just practical action; how else could a man carry a woman on his 60-kilometre walk? He has swaddled her so well; how do some men know how to wrap their dead wives? Has he done this before? His daughter was in a salwar-kameez, which we can guess is not the type of garment her mother would have ever worn. That must be progress. They lived somewhere in the Kalahandi district of Odisha, one of the poorest regions of India.

Hours after Dana was photographed, the image entered social media. Urban Indians were disturbed. But this was not the biggest news of the day. The top news was the sedition charge against an Indian actress for saying that Pakistan was ‘not a hell’. But India was not so crass that it was not news at all. It made it to the inside pages of most newspapers. In response, the Odisha government first blamed him for walking away with the body, then grudgingly gave him money for his wife’s funeral. (The Bahrain government donated about a million rupees. Eventually there was another photograph of Dana—in shirt and trousers on a new motorbike, smiling. He soon remarried, too. His neighbours were bitter about his luck. They said he was a changed man. ‘Dana is not the same Dana now. He got all the benefits while we got nothing,’ a neighbour said.)

Dana is not a mascot of Indian poverty. At the time his wife died, he was perhaps poorer than most Indians. But he does represent the meekness of India’s poor. A system was unkind to him, and he knew it as a way of life. He did not know his rights, or that he had some rights. He just carried his wife and walked. He was a mentally sound man. There was something about his action that seemed very natural to him. As though he had seen other men do it, or maybe he himself had done somewhat similar actions at this level before. There is a certain antiquity in this. Most Indians may not have to do anything like what Dana did, but they do accept their own tragedies in his manner. As though in the bad things that happen to them, there are no villains.

Most Indians are poor by the standards of the modern world. If a rural household earns about ₹100,000 to ₹125,000 a month, they would be in the top 5 per cent of Indians. India has, indeed, pulled millions ‘out of poverty’, partly through progress, but chiefly through statistics and by having a poor man’s definition of poverty. For instance, if you can afford to spend about ₹50 a day, in a city, or about ₹30 in a village, you would be ‘above’ the poverty line. An Indian household is a statistical unit of roughly five Indians. This means if an entire urban household, say in Mumbai or Delhi, can afford to spend ₹7,500 a month (on its five members), it would not be considered poor.

The poverty of average Indians is a reason why most of my readers have a good life. It is very cheap to be middle class in India. Because the poor subsidize us all. They force the government and the markets to keep the prices of essentials, including water and electricity, very low, which we consume many times more than the average Indian. But the chief reason why India is a middle-class paradise and why our friends suffer when they go to the West is that the poor serve us for almost nothing. As I write this, I let a man into my home to ‘deep clean’ the bathroom. I had booked him on UrbanClap. He will clean my bathroom for eighty minutes with a machine, and using chemicals he bought at his expense—for ₹500, of which he would receive ₹380. Many times, my Zomato order is more than my maid’s monthly pay, and I have developed the habit of snatching the parcel before she can see the bill. When I order Atlantic salmon, I wait at the door as though it is porn. I rip price tags off clothes because I do not want the maid to get mad. You may say they know; but there is a good chance they don’t know just how unequal India is. When I was in my twenties, I had a notion of the incomes of my bosses and of the rich. I realize now that I was way off the mark. If I had known, maybe, I would have rioted in some form. (About a decade later I became an editor myself, earning much more than what my colleagues imagined. One day, I made a managerial error trying to retain a senior guy who wished to quit. I almost doubled his pay. He looked at me with hate because he had figured that I was earning more than twice his pay. It was be a look I never saw in the peons.)

India’s poor are not only poor, they are poor in one of the most unequal regions on Earth. All around them, there is money sloshing about. I live in a colony that probably has the per capita income of an advanced nation. The tailor in the local market alters trousers for fifty rupees; I have seen some people try to bargain it down.

Some of India’s poor do react violently to the inequality. The poor do kill. In 2016, my friend was raped and murdered in her home, in Goa. The murderer was a ‘security guard’, one of those poor men whom we squeeze into the fancy dress of a paramilitary outfit. He had held her responsible for his sacking. He hid on the terrace of the apartment block and eventually gained entry into her house. After the murder, he stole her ATM card and was so naive that he actually used it—the reason why he was nabbed within days of the crime.

There are daily robberies and crime born out of hate and deprivation. Even so, India is one of the safest nations for the middle classes and the rich. That is odd. When I say, ‘why the poor don’t kill us’, I do mean why they do not rise in violent revolt, but I also mean it as a form of wonder. Also, I mean to ask why they don’t overthrow us in non-violent ways, in ways that are not known as revolts. But even in this form of revolt they are, mysteriously, not as aggressive as they should be. It is as though they are overrating our forts, our power to fight back.



CHAPTER 2: WHY THE POOR DON’T KILL US
-------------------------------------

WHEN I was in college, I read Nani Palkhivala, who was always described as an ‘eminent jurist’. I had a lot of respect then for anyone who was called ‘eminent’. I was twenty-one when I finally met him. He was by then an old man. He told me India’s poor were wonderful people. He conveyed that nonsensical praise rich Indians and foreigners often give the poor—‘so hospitable’. The eminent jurist turned out to be yet another affluent Indian simpleton. The inner beauty of India’s poor is a popular mainstream opinion. There would be many Indians, today, who would with flared noble nostrils say the poor do not kill the rich because the poor are such wonderful folk. It is not that I dispute this view; it is just that the visible niceness of the poor is nothing virtuous—it is a consequence of compulsion. As in love, so in society, people who do not have power are quite nice. And in the case of the poor, it is another layer of retardation inflicted on them through very low self-worth.

A bit of that submission of the poor is also plain acting. As foolish as it is to assume that the poor are made of milk and honey, is the suspicion of rich India, expressed and secret, that the poor are prone to criminality and violence, and that they are kept in check only by religious or other mystical forces, and of course, the fear of the law. In my view, human beings are not intrinsically good or evil. I think they are intrinsically cautious. This caution has the appearance of a virtue, or maybe it really is a virtue. Maybe that is all there is to being humane—a sort of caution. Whatever it is, it is good enough for peace. It is good enough for India’s poor to shun violence or even abhor it. But it is also true that the poor are reined in by powerful forces, like the many leashes of morality, and the promises of hope that the rich have consciously and unconsciously designed to control the poor, to make them fall in line.

There are reasons why the poor don’t revolt that are not so obvious. In this book, I chiefly discuss those reasons. Let’s begin.



How India’s chaos and ugliness protect us

The Arun Jaitley stadium in Delhi looks so third-rate and ugly, it would reassure 95 per cent of Indians that the nation has not left them behind—something the suave former finance minister himself could not do in his lifetime. Almost all of India is like this.

Most nations try hard to look richer than they are; India, in plain sight, is a lot poorer than it is in reality. This municipal ugliness of India conveys to the poor that the nation belongs to them, that it has not left them behind. The street chaos and our general civic disorder, too, have the same effect.

Exquisite urban beauty or postmodern design, on the other hand, make the poor of that society feel worse than they should, and that they are encroaching on the fiefdom of better people. In the discontent of advanced economies and upper-middle-income nations, some blame should go to architectural glitter.

I do not have any romantic association with disorder and ugliness. I hate that about India. I just say they play a part in peace, for that is the nature of human beings.



The poor are the worst enemies of the poor

Like us, the poor have a theory of whom they must hate—‘the big people’. But, like us, the people they hate the most are their equals—their neighbours and relatives and others like them. Poor women face the worst harassment from poor men. Poor men face destructive competition from other poor men. The poor do not envy the rich. That notion arises from a common fallacy of how envy works. Envy is a thing between equals. The poor envy others like them who have done better. Many Indian caste atrocities emerge from ordinary envy between the poor. The poor migrant’s worst enemy is the local poor for it is him the migrant undercuts.

Meanwhile, the rich do beautiful things for the poor—they make lovely movies about poverty, they feed the poor, and indignant young women from good families speak of so many humane things.



The absence of human rights for the poor protects us

In some aspects, India is safe not only compared to middle-income countries in Africa and Latin America, but also in comparison with the West. And that is because of an Indian quality that is not exactly noble, which is also why it works. What protects us is the brutal side of India. If at all there is anything worse than poverty, it is an Indian jail and to be caught in the Indian judicial system for criminal activity. It is a deadly deterrent. Also, criminals and rapists of children are routinely bumped off, and the society is not interested in investigating the circumstances that led to their ‘encounters’. What India cannot do with competent legal systems, it accomplishes in informal ways.

Also, Indian social life, especially in the under classes, is a natural filtering process. People who are dangerous, or even mentally ill, do not survive too long. They are killed or apprehended soon after they become a danger. Or, they just die alone of neglect and disease. In comparison, dangerous people can survive in more compassionate societies like the United States and Europe.



How politicians calm the poor

For decades, the educated view has been that the leopard wished to be in the forest, and that it entered human habitats because of circumstances, like shrinking forests. So, wildlife officials capture leopards that have ‘strayed’ into human territories and release them in the wild.

But now the academic opinion has shifted. The leopard considers itself ‘a resident’ of spaces that humans have occupied. It is not straying. It finds human settlements conducive to a quality life, with their dogs and cattle. Humans themselves, though delectable perhaps, are dangerous.

The fact is that there is no such thing as a ‘natural’ habitat. The leopards (and also the lions in some regions of Gujarat), are increasingly showing that their ‘natural’ habitat is wherever there is abundant food. In 2002, I went to meet some leopards in Junnar, a sugarcane district about 200 km away from Mumbai. At the time of my visit, leopards had attacked twenty- two people. In the three months before I visited, there had been eight deaths, mostly children. The cats had consumed nearly 700 cattle in the previous two-and- a-half years. Nobody counted the dogs that had gone missing.

The people in the region wanted the government to kill the leopards. The trouble was, the deputy conservator of forests in Junnar, Ashok Khadse, told me, ‘I am paid to protect the leopard.’ The villagers were furious about this fact. Forest officials did what they were supposed to do —they captured the leopards and released them into the forest. But the leopards kept returning, with their friends and lovers. They loved the place. The sugarcane farms provided good cover, and as it was one of the most irrigated places in the state, there was abundant water. And, of course, there were cattle and dogs, and little unattended humans.

The villagers wanted the leopards killed not only to save their own lives but also to avenge past killings. (In 2022, villagers in Uttarakhand burned a captured leopard alive in its cage, to avenge the death of a woman it had killed.) But forest officials, who were forced to live in a modern era by Europe-grade laws for animals, said they would protect the leopards. They asked the villagers to grow sugarcane in patches, to deny leopards vast cover. But then sugarcane is a lucrative crop and the farmers wanted to grow it on every inch possible. They were aghast that the government was going to such lengths to protect the leopards.

A few days before I arrived, five-year-old Manisha Doke was killed by a leopard. Her distraught father, Sanjay Doke, a farmworker, told me, ‘Should we go to the forest so that the leopard can live here?’ He was due to receive compensation of ₹20,000 for the death of his daughter. The penalty for killing a leopard, at the time of my visit, was ₹25,000.

Things had come to such a pass that if anyone went missing in the region, they first suspected the leopards. Some of the missing turned out to be lovers who had eloped. But the fact was that there were frequent attacks and deaths, and loss of cattle too. The villagers were so angry that forest officials had to call the cops to escort tranquillized leopards to the forest. Soon, politicians moved in. The Shiv Sena led demonstrations demanding the execution of the leopards. This emerged from the most important moral greyness of Indian politicians.

India’s laws that protected the leopards were exemplary, correct, moral, and very wise. But the villagers who faced the threat every day could not afford to be so noble. They knew that a section of influential people were not fully with them—the forest officials and the police had to follow the law; the journalists were on the side of the leopards (as it was a time when there were only liberal journalists). The government itself, in practice, was on the side of the leopards. But the politicians alone had the crudity to speak the minds of the villagers. It comforted the villagers.

Leopard attacks continue in the region to this day. It is possible that a whole generation of leopards have never seen a forest; they were born in the sugarcane farms and grew up in the region. The politicians still articulate the emotions of the villagers. As happens often in India, they may not have solved a complex issue, but they act as vents for anger so that it does not escalate into something worse. They also protect the poor from the amateur Indian who, with his obtuse aping of Western ideals, harms the poor. For instance, India’s privacy warriors wanted to abolish India’s plan to give every Indian a unique identity number. The politicians saved the plan and it is today, despite its problems, the most efficient way for the government to transfer cash directly to the poor.

Time and again, India encounters man-woke conflicts. A highly ethical global idea, a sophisticated idea, infuriates simple people who have to bear its consequences. And politicians alone, in self-interest, state what is unsayable.



Vulgarity is also hope

A benign fraud in art and journalism is contrast photography, the kind where the contrast is not a property of light, but of India. Burqa-clad women walking beside girls in small skirts; the sari-draped scientists of the space programme; a Bharata Natyam dancer holding a laptop. A meaningful question that we can ask the creators of such frames is: ‘So?’ There are no contradictions in these images. Some contrast photography, though, conveys vulgarity, and it may have a point. For instance, a naked urchin at the giant glass window of an upscale restaurant; the homeless beneath a builder’s hoarding. India is filled with this sort of vulgarity. But it is not always bad. In some forms of vulgarity, there is also hope.

This is what intellectuals didn’t get about the richest Indian Mukesh Ambani (and American intellectuals did not understand about Donald Trump). People, today, aspire to money and not class. They admire money, even if it is on other people. When Ambani, the richest Indian, built one of the most expensive homes in the world, a twenty-seven storey-high modern palace in the heart of Mumbai, the intellectuals said it was self-destructive for him to mock the average Indian in this way. But the average Indian did not feel mocked; they saw hope. It was a shrine to a commodity that they thought they, too, can amass. (It was the old-money millionaires of Mumbai who resented Ambani—for diminishing their sea views.)

As we will see, there is a sort of vulgarity that is dangerous in a poor country, but there is an aspirational quality to some kinds of vulgarity.



The false hope of higher education

The poor, who wish to make something of the lives of their children, are sucked into the powerful idea of education. Those who aspire for high-level jobs only realize much later that material success, except for geniuses in sports, is largely about social class. Still, their co-option in the form of education is valuable to them. Their lives do become somewhat better because the modern economic system is rigged to favour the educated. As a result, the poor play a game where the elite hold all the cards. They feel that if they do a set of things, if they toe the line, they will be offered a chance at a better life. This is not entirely untrue. Of course, the poor cannot unseat the rich in some lucrative fields like heart surgery, corporate law, investment banking, or the whole start-up culture where social class is crucial. But the rich do keep vacating many other spheres as they move on to newer professions or intellectual pursuits, or romanticized forms of unemployment. The educated among the poor then fill up the spaces the rich have vacated.



A theory about how change happens

Many people think that in a revolution, the enraged poor would run out of their homes holding an improvised weapon, and overthrow the rich and the ruling class. But I cannot think of a revolution where this actually happened, at least this way. Even the French Revolution, where we get the idea of pitchforks coming for the rich, was not that at all. Every revolution is created and sponsored by the second rung of the elite to trounce their oppressor, nominally in the interest of things like equality and fairness. The wars against capitalism, inequality, dictatorship, and men are primarily the wars of the top 2 per cent against the top 1 per cent, with everyone else enlisted in the cause of the second rung.

This is true even of the Indian freedom movement. Most of the icons of the Indian freedom movement were from the highest caste, relegated to a social rung below the British. We can frame the freedom movement as the aristocrat’s use of morality to mobilize the masses and defeat his oppressor.

We see this phenomenon at play today in all of activism. It is always moral, always for a better world, always a war of one rung of the elite against their oppressor but fought through recruits. The aristocratic handlers of revolutions are often not charlatans. They believe that they are not acting in self-interest, but out of intelligent compassion.

That is why they are so effective. Thus, so many millionaires are waging wars against billionaires for a better world. And, in some ways, the world does become better.



The miserable are not as miserable as we think

Our darkest fears come true in some people, and we expect them to be miserable. But are the unlucky as miserable as other people think? Is it possible that the bereaved and the disabled, the jailed and the whores, and most people who are said to be tragic, are somewhat better than what the others think? Can it be that the poor, too, are fine and that they may even be happy? After all, an unsung cause of human happiness is low standards. For friends, for the spouse, for the nation. Maybe, just like some people find misery in everything, most people are programmed to find joy in their lives no matter what. Maybe, the poor are not as miserable as we fear. The persistence of happiness, the inevitability of happiness, maybe that, too, protects us.

IN this book, you will read more about these reasons, and several other reasons that are yet to be mentioned, all of them explaining what keeps the peace between the classes.

But this peace is fragile.



CHAPTER 3: HOW WE PROVOKE
-------------------------

INDIA’S public character comes from its poor. India is the way it is, chiefly because of its poor. On its part, India treats its poor like they are poor. This is India’s central flaw. The poor have very low standards for themselves. They can squeeze into a steaming, humid, unreserved train compartment, so many of them per square foot that if they were cows it would be illegal to transport so many in that space, and they can travel like this for days. They even travel inside cement-mixers. They can wait for a bus for hours. They walk extraordinary distances in the sun, carrying heavy objects and humans, if they don’t have a choice. As a reporter, I used to frequently encounter an attitude that concerned air conditioning. That the poor do not ‘deserve’ air conditioning. Not only because they will not pay for it, but also because they do not expect to be so well treated. Almost all of India is hot most of the year, and the summers are scorching and long, yet most offices and modes of public transport are not air-conditioned. India is not protecting the environment; just that it perceives air conditioning as a luxury, and its unspoken policy is that Indians need not be treated with luxuries. That is how India has trained the poor to think, and this is how the poor have shaped India to treat them.

In the heart of all the good that India tries to do—not only through policy but also through pious activism—there is this unspoken sense that low standards ‘will do’ for the poor. India’s very definition of comfort is a state that is inaccessible to the poor.

The low standards for the poor are embedded in all the cures that India prescribes for poverty. It begins in the faulty middle-class academic conjecture of what the poor want. There is this consecration of food, a certain cultural oversensitivity to the act of eating. Not a surprise, then, that the moment there is a crisis, the government announces that every poor person will get bags of starch.

The poor have shaped India in their own image. Every Indian who is not poor is, inescapably, a poverty-eradication thinker. His insurmountable problem is finding a competent authority to whom to delegate this task.

Almost every concrete and abstract thing in India is influenced by the poor. India’s elite in every field are deeply influenced and shaped by the poor. India’s poverty gives Indians a clear moral direction. Every affluent Indian may or may not have a moral compass, but certainly has one for India, and it directs the country to end poverty. How to go about it is at the heart of all our economic, social, and political debates. People who do not consider this the priority cannot hope for a career in public life, or economics, or even find acclaim in the arts.

Despite this attitude towards the poor and poverty by the better off, or perhaps because of it, we are, in many ways, a vulgar nation. Earlier in the book, I talked of why I am not against all vulgarity. It is complex and, in certain situations, useful. Some of it is even inevitable. Even so, we are a vulgar nation in a shameful way.

Consider the Indian five-star buffet.

If you want to make Indians laugh, show them what Europeans call a ‘buffet’. Even at a high-end hotel there, the spread is so small it would fit into a single Indian glance, which by nature is sweeping. On the other hand, in most of Asia, especially India, the five-star buffet is designed to convey that the hotel has made a loss on it. The buffet is so sprawling that one has to first circumnavigate the spread, even get the lay of the land. Still there would be surprises, nooks from where food emerges and some things that are not displayed but can be requested or extorted.

There is something very wrong with the Indian buffet. To understand why this is so, meander with me a bit, as though we are at a vast buffet of Indian maladies. The Indian buffet is, primarily, a spectacle. Especially the people who are there to eat it—some of them are in their pyjamas. They feel at home; they are eating in the same address where they sleep, and they pay to sleep here, and that must be a definition of home. So they appear in clothes they would normally never wear if they were going to be in the line of vision of a hundred strangers. I always try to ascertain if people who are in pyjamas are also wearing underwear. The finding is that people who appear in pyjamas at the buffet do take the trouble of wearing various forms of bolstering inner wear to be presentable, but in their outerwear they want the comfort of elastic around their stomachs during this limitless meal. Feeling at home, they perceive hotel staff as ‘servants’.

There is so much choice in an Indian buffet that people are able to fill their plates according to their deep mental states. The plates of large people are usually filled with fruits, which is usually their first course when in public. They are also happy to display a plate of fruits, unlike the later trips that are more discreet. Mostly, people fill their plates with bread.

The whole buffet scene is like a rave party, with distressed waiters dealing in sugar and refined flour; and the addicts wanting more and more, shouting for the attention of the dealers, asking why something is taking so long, how long must they wait; how long must their poor starving children wait before the next fix. The anxious addicts of food are aware that waiters here are too powerless to ever tell them, ‘It’s a buffet you fat fuck, go and take it yourself.’

The buffet is an overt encouragement to eat as much as you can, which means consume more than you need to. Everything about food and feeding in our country is about excess.

At the end of it all, the Indian buffet is meant to be wasted. Some people in the hotel industry tell me that half of the food in a typical buffet is wasted. There is a certain vulgarity in excess and wastage, especially in a poor country. This vulgarity is different from a skyscraper home or a luxury car, which create the myth of aspiration—the notion that if anyone does a set of things, or if they are ‘smart’, they can achieve it. But wastage of food is visually repulsive to the poor, the real poor—not the European poor, but the sort of poor India has in millions, from whose midst a hotel’s kitchen worker or waiter hail.

Consider a massive Indian five-star buffet from the point of view of a new waiter who has arrived from a distant impoverished village. After he recovers from the fact that a single meal in the hotel costs as much as half his salary, perhaps, imagine what goes on in his head when he sees people taking heaps of delicious things and leaving it all behind, or the hotel throws away hundreds of kilos of perfectly edible food? It is vulgar, and this sort of vulgarity has no redeeming qualities.

Often, vulgarity itself has no consequence for us. We can’t help some of it, but still the fact is that there is a kind of vulgarity that is a form of disrespect of an unlucky person.



Why you are more vulgar than a billionaire

Everyone finds someone else vulgar. For example, the Indian middle class find billionaires vulgar. Extravagant weddings of the super-rich, for instance, never fail to repulse a type of affluent, refined Indian even though he may not always be from the South. Too golden, too grand, too garish, he would complain. How can you spend so much money when there is ‘agrarian distress’? The urban middle class does not employ the poor merely to cook for them, drive them, and walk their dogs, but also to be used as moral pedestals to stand on and accuse billionaires that they are ‘too vulgar in a poor country’. But then, do India’s vast poor make a serious distinction between the billionaires, the centi-millionaires, the multi-millionaires, the mere millionaires, the asset-millionaires, the soon-to-inherit- dad’s-house millionaires, and the rest of the top 1 per cent? Aren’t the Škodas of the conscientious ‘austere’ Indians as gaudy as the Maybachs in the eyes of the starving? And aren’t the elegant and spare soirées on the lawns as vulgar as the mega-weddings in the eyes of gaping drivers? And the disdain that Arundhati Roy has for Mukesh Ambani’s giant home—wouldn’t a malnourished tribal feel the same about Roy’s affluent home in Delhi’s prime Jor Bagh?

Also, what is the show of wealth the poor actually see? To them it is the middle class that is the most visible section of ‘the rich’. Because the ionosphere of the super-rich is beyond what their eyes can see. (This is what commentators missed in the aftermath of ‘demonetization’ when they did not see its political popularity—they didn’t see why the cancelling of large denomination notes, the confusion that created, and the long queues outside ATMs actually conveyed to the average Indian, at that time, that there was a war against the ‘corrupt rich’. In the public misery of the middle class, the average voter saw the rare fall of the rich.)

The vulgarity of super-rich weddings should ideally suggest to the Indian middle class how vulgar they themselves appear to the poor. The classy think their wealth is hidden from plain sight, but there are things that are not invisible to the poor. In fact, class reminds them of both wealth and alien culture, the reason why class is politically more despicable than son-of-the-soil wealth. Isn’t it true, after all, that the average South Indian relates more to a Rajinikanth, the multi-millionaire, than to any of their region’s ‘austere’ intellectuals whose only extravagance is the invisible money their parents had spent on their college degrees? The average voter despises the articulate global intellectual; so much that even when the intellectual talks sense, the voter hates sense itself. From this faultline flows a stream of politics.

YOU may argue that the poor can tell the difference between you and a billionaire who commutes in choppers. I grant this, though I would find it hilarious to tell a poor man, ‘Wealth, my friend, is a spectrum’.

Is poverty a spectrum? There can be a spectrum of data, but is the state of being poor a spectrum? In politics, identity is never a spectrum. To be precise, spectrum always loses to identity that is not ambiguous. Political identity is often binary. Male, female. White, black. Rich, poor. There was a time when the Indian middle class behaved like the poor (many of us went to ration shops, we went to the same schools, and watched the same film as the poor in the same theatre and probably voted for the same political party, the Indian National Congress); but today we behave more like the rich. A person always knows the precise answer to the question: In your nation are you rich or poor?

There are millions of Indians who feel they are poor. So far, they have revolted against us through politics. But in the future there will be other ways. The provocations are there, every day.

LIGHT does not pass through the Indian maid, even though many Indians may want that. Light strikes her and is reflected back to create one of the most inconvenient apparitions for affluent Indians. That was always the property of light, but somehow there was a time, not very long ago, when the maids knew how to be invisible. They would stay in the kitchen, merge with the backdrop, walk without noise, never speak unless they were answering a question. But now they take breaks, talk on their phones with friends, walk in the parks of their masters. As a result, they have entered India’s ongoing servant–master conflicts.

In 2023, a somewhat affluent residential society in Bengaluru issued a statement: ‘It’s difficult to see them (maids) hang out everywhere in the park, amphitheatre and gazebos. Residents can feel uncomfortable when being surrounded by maids everywhere we walk… Cooks, carpenters, plumbers sit on the sofa at the building reception. Most of us have probably stopped sitting on the sofas by now…’

When this leaked, there was familiar outrage. India appears to be such a fair place on social media. Many people pointed out that ‘the maids’ were ‘human too’. I find this taunt odd. The Indian upper class knows ‘servants’ are humans. They have problems only with humans. If, say, deer walked around their parks and amphitheatre and gazebos, and sat on their sofas, they would find it very cute.

What the residential society was trying to arrive at was that the poor bring down the aesthetics of the place. What use is all the landscaping, when the poor come in a pall of visual poverty? And, because of mobile phones, they are not only seen but also heard— laughing, crying, fighting, loving. They also hog the meagre private resources of a community whose nation gives them nothing in the way of beauty. This is a nation where the middle class pays a premium not for the quality of things but to ensure the other kind of Indians are not allowed entry.

The society’s notice is a part of the ongoing effort of the middle class to restrict the visibility of the poor in their colonies. Not only is the effort doomed to fail, it creates a new class of degradation of aesthetics—like notices and noticeboards that ask ‘servants and dogs’ not to take the lift. Class is ugly everywhere, but unlike in the West, Indians somehow have not figured out how to hide the ugliness.

Even as India’s affluent worry about the increasing visibility of the support staff, they cannot survive without them. They are addicted to them as they have been for a long time.

I live in a beautiful colony in Gurugram (erstwhile Gurgaon) that has broad, tree-lined pathways, parks with flower beds, and little green mounds. Old men amble about in groups talking about the importance of authoritarianism; their wives sit in a circle and sing. An American with a mohawk teaches karate to teenaged girls; and some boys are so posh they can’t bowl. Here, I have heard boys rib each other saying, ‘he kissed an EWS girl’, meaning economically weaker section girl. It is one of those places where Indians escape India. Yet, every morning India storms the gates.

First, scores of security guards troop in, almost all of them migrants from the poorest North Indian states. Then dozens of dog-walkers, who are almost all male and chiefly Bangla migrants, arrive. Most of them despise dogs. They walk dragging the animals, talking to other dog-walker friends, or in the trance of their phones, or flirting with the rare female dog-walkers, who are usually from the Northeast. Then the part-time maids come on their bicycles, to be harassed by the guards who think they are flirting. Then the drivers come, the segment that is for some mysterious reason the most self-assured. Most of them have very little work, and they linger about playing cards with other drivers, or stand on the road and peer into homes. Meanwhile, the colony invests a lot of effort in watering parks and creating flower beds and doing other such things in its futile attempt to elevate the aesthetics of the whole place.

When I was married, I used to live in a very Indian way with resident servants. Every morning, I would return from a run to find a poor person squatting on the floor, mopping it. To save myself from this apparition I offered the long-handled mop. I used to offer it to every new maid. I gave up because I think I was beginning to look creepy; also they don’t like the more dignified mop, for some reason. In the kitchen, a melancholic cook would be strangling dough. I felt like I was living with two poor cousins. A strange nature of India’s affluent is that even as they worry about their support staff bringing down the aesthetics of their habitats, they do not do much to help them look better. So we often see in malls and airports the Indian family trailed by their pale maid, who is in shabby clothes. It would not cost much to groom them, but somehow we do not do that.

The typical Indian house help looks far more impoverished than, say, the Filipina maid who is a familiar sight in places like Dubai and Singapore. I came to know of a non-resident Indian couple who landed in Delhi with their Filipina maid. The whole extended family was abuzz with descriptions of the ‘very sexy’ maid, who ‘looked’ more affluent than my relatives whom she was serving. It appears that India’s support staff are chosen in a Darwinian selection to ensure they do not threaten their memsahibs or arouse the egalitarian muscle in the sahibs.

SOME things that my relatives do every day are crimes in Europe. I was reminded of it when news broke that four members of the billionaire Hinduja family were held guilty by a Swiss court of treating their house staff badly. The prosecution said that the Hindujas paid the staff about $8 a day and that their workdays could be as long as eighteen hours.

It is likely that the Indian upper class sees all this legal action as excessive because many of them make poor people work for more than twelve hours on less than $8 a day. In their defence, they would say that the cost of living in India is a few times lower than in Europe, so what household staff members are paid in India is not as bad as it sounds.

A prosecutor said that the family had spent almost $10,000 a year on their dog, while paying low wages to their human staff. This could be true even in a typical upper-middle-class Indian household. They may want to point out, though they would never do so in public, that do-gooders have a bad habit of comparing how much a family spends on their dog with how much they pay their domestic workers. The comparison is absurd. A dog brings a lot of joy to the family, and is even seen as a part of the family, unlike an employee.

The Hindujas also pointed out that their domestic staff’s compensation was not just the cash they received, but also accommodation and food. That is exactly what my relatives, too, would say. ‘Accommodation’ in an Indian household is usually a room that appears to be purposely impoverished. In my colony, for instance, the default ‘servant quarters’ is a windowless room fit for suitcases.

As a boy growing up in Madras, the most fascinating thing I heard about the world outside was that in the US, garbage cleaners would come in cars to clean the streets. That remains, to date, the most impressive thing I have ever heard about wealth. I eventually realized that the information, in word and spirit, was a bit of an exaggeration. But the idea stuck that the best measure of a nation’s wealth is the standard of living of its poor. Ideally, the measure of an individual’s wealth should be how much they pay their house staff.


    ‘How rich is that guy?’ ‘Oh, he pays his driver 100,000 bucks a month.’

I really did think in my twenties that was how the world worked. The rich are willing to talk about how much they spend on cars and carpets and homes. Wouldn’t it be interesting to know, on some show, how much the super-rich spend on their servants?

For some reason, you can get to know a lot about obscure things that the rich spend on, but never what they spend on their staff. That is because most rich people, like the Hindujas, pay only as much as what the middle class pays their household staff. In my colony, domestic workers are paid half of Europe’s minimum wage. I am not lamenting this. After all, I too pay my cleaner ₹5,000 a month for getting into acrobatic positions to clean for an hour every day, and a cook only around the same for working sixty minutes daily on meals.

The rich keep talking about how they subsidize the poor. They do. But it is also a fact that the poor subsidize our lifestyles by supplying labour cheaper than in much of the world. Also, every layer of Indian society subsidizes the lifestyle of the layer above by working for exploitatively low wages. And in the end, we have a situation where Indian billionaires probably pay only as much as I do for domestic work.

As a result, India is among the unhappiest places on Earth. This was probably the case for the longest time, but for long it could hide behind a vacuous myth created by the hippies that this place was filled with some abstract, innate joy. It was a beloved belief of the upper class, but it was rubbish. Most Indians were miserable; no one asked them. Until someone found a way to.



Among the unhappiest nations on Earth

Over the past decade, a global survey has been asking thousands of people across the world how happy they are on a scale of 0 to 10, in six distinct ways of being happy. Their answers form the core of the annual World Happiness Reports, which have now become a source of periodic unhappiness for many patriotic Indians. India is consistently ranked at the bottom, clubbed with dystopian nations. The 2025 World Happiness Report ranks India 118th out of 147 nations, worse than Ukraine, Iraq, and Pakistan, which is a cause of special trauma for sensitive Indians. Any measure of happiness involves a few foolish processes, and these reports are guilty of passing off righteousness as anthropology, but they are less nonsensical than a fable my generation grew up hearing: that Indians are ‘the happiest people on Earth’.

When I was a kid, Indians told each other that the rich were unhappy. They said it with a touch of jubilation. Because the rich were other people, who went about in cars, and most Indians were closer to the poor than to the rich. In more than one Malayalam film, the upscale woman wore a sleeveless blouse, smoked, and stared long at sleeping pills on her palm at night. We didn’t know the origins of her grief; she was this way at the start of the film. We were expected to understand that her problem was the state of being a rich urban woman. The sleeping pill of the 80s became, in the 90s, Art of Living classes and the philosophies of men who said, ‘I am you and you are me’, yet wore fancy dress costumes to distinguish themselves from you and me.

They mined a myth Indians were trained from birth to believe—that we had an innate spiritual power that no one else possessed, some great ancient secret that the West came seeking. Every now and then, a public figure would speak of the unhappy West and our joyful poor. In a Hindi film, a character scoffs at the loneliness of Americans who have ‘everything’ but do not have family or friends, while admiring Indians who have nothing but bask in a swarm of people. The theatre erupted in applause as though it was a great moment of insight. In the age of social media, we often see posts claiming to be true portraits of the happiness of poor Indians. These feature images of the poor diving into a pond or enjoying rain (somehow water is vital to joy in poor nations). All through this farce, the truth is that India has been among the most miserable places on Earth. As an Indian resident who has travelled to the West, I find it easy to believe the broad conclusions of the World Happiness Report—that Scandinavians are the likeliest to rate themselves as happy (and that suicide rates say nothing about happiness in any region), and that the average Indian is among the world’s least happy. This rings untrue to affluent and middle-class Indians because they are among the happiest. They would score well on a happiness survey that asks people to rate their own happiness in six aspects of life: the social support of peers, financial well-being, health, the freedom to make important decisions, the generosity of society, and the absence of corruption. India’s upper class would rate themselves at Scandinavian levels on at least the first four parameters.

Their bafflement at India’s rank among the most miserable is actually predicted by the happiness report in one of its more credible exercises—the measure of inequality in self-evaluated joy. To measure this form of inequality, the report takes the happiest half of a sample in a region and the unhappiest half, then considers the gap that separates them. In Finland, ranked the happiest nation in the world, the gap between the top half and bottom half is narrow. This means there is a near uniformity in how people rate themselves. Several affluent democratic nations demonstrate such small differences. But the nation that is most equal in its self-evaluation of happiness is not a happily rich nation but Afghanistan, which has to be among the world’s most miserable places. This makes sense. In Afghanistan, the happiness of the top half is not much higher than that of the bottom half. In other words, the country is almost uniformly miserable.

In India, people who rate themselves as happy are much happier than those who say they are unhappy. This shows up a country where life is very good for some and miserable for the poor; a region where it is vastly advantageous to be born in the right home, and a severe handicap not to be so lucky.

If your memory is as good as mine, there was a time when intellectuals promoted Bhutan as the happiest place on Earth. In the 2013 Hindi film, The Lunchbox, an unhappy woman in Mumbai plots to flee to Bhutan in search of joy. It was the Bhutanese king Jigme Singye Wangchuck who popularized the idea of ‘Gross National Happiness’ in 1972, when his reign began. In the 2000s, this abstract idea caught the imagination of liberal economists who thought they had found a tool to defame the focus on economic growth. But, it turns out, Bhutanese people themselves do not rate themselves very highly on the happiness scale. The first World Happiness Report, published in 2012, pays rich tribute to Bhutan through the scholars who crafted the report to rebuke capitalism, but the average Bhutanese themselves ranked their nation low. Apparently, the Bhutanese are not as happy as everybody thought. It is Western Europe that has consistently regarded itself happy.

The moral of the story is that the secret to happiness may lie in colonizing, brutalizing, and plundering vast regions of the world so that you can one day raise a financially pampered and socially compassionate population that cycles to work.



CHAPTER 4: HOW INDIA’S CHAOS AND UGLINESS REASSURE THE POOR
-----------------------------------------------------------

THERE is a difference in the circumstances of European nobodies and Indian nobodies. In Western Europe, the nobodies are in ghettos of abject failure surrounded by a mainstream society that is, in plain sight, happy and thriving. The city centres are gorgeous, joyous, usually ancient, and a creation of the same race that is the economically dominant one today. All public places look a lot better than the private spaces of the poor. The poor in a rich country are like a bitter Reddit page trapped inside Instagram. Grand symbols of progress, like giant cable-stayed bridges and beautiful buildings, visually emphasize the failures of the losers. In the new disenchantment among Western Europe’s poor, such as in France and Belgium, we should not underestimate the role of the spectacular urban beauty of the great cities in the region. Even though the poor of Europe enjoy a quality of public life that is, in some regards, superior to the private lives of Indian millionaires in Mumbai or Gurugram, they cannot escape the gloom of being in proximity to a glowing mainstream. In a rich country, those who consider themselves poor feel that the nation has left them behind. Their poverty always has a human villain.

The Indian nobodies see a very different world. They, of course, see the glitter of the rich, the decadence of the young in long cars, and the illuminated fine-dining behind glass windows, but it is the rich who are in their cages, and it is the classy who are confused about their idea of home. Most of the nation belongs to the nobodies; it is a republic of nobodies.

India’s roads are among the freest places on Earth. There is a national feeling that lanes are imaginary lines, like the equator. People drive on the wrong side of the road and abuse you for not giving way. Google Maps itself has been so confused by Indians that it has asked me, at least once, to drive on the wrong side. Yet, Indians are surprised when things go wrong. Observe the guy who has been driving like a moron and has finally crashed—he is stunned. People cross the road like cows and they are baffled by the appearance of a car—a car on the road, how? People cross the railway track at will, and they are shocked to see a train thundering towards them. A train on a railway track, how? Surprise is our national emotion; surprise at the most logical outcome of our actions. Maybe Indians are surprised because at some deep, unconscious level there is an elegant reason why they chose disorder over order, and were not expecting punishment for it.

India’s public chaos, like its architectural ugliness, convey to the poor that the nation is something like them—poor. This is not entirely admirable. India’s disorderly roads kill thousands every year in road accidents. Hoardings just fall on people walking on the road. Every now and then, in temples and on railway stations, stampedes kill scores of people. Hundreds fall off trains every day, or are run over by them. All this is because India is not serious about rules and safety protocols, not serious about order. India is many times more serious about social order than public order. But this informal quality is also a relief to the average Indian.

Actually, it is not only the poor whom India’s chaos calms. The fact is, India’s elite, too, love their nation’s chaos more than they admit. They would have liked their cities to be more beautiful, at least as well tended as their residential colonies, but the disorder of India, they are not repulsed enough by that.

In any First World city, the edges of a road are never crooked, and median markings, which have clear meanings, run straight, forever. People who have driving licences actually know how to drive. Their cars go in lanes, which are not imaginary lines. Even round a sharp bend, cars stay in their lane as though in orbit around some unseen centre of sophistication. And they stop at the red light behind a straight line. And when they do, there is so much silence and stillness, I often mistake them for parked cars. And, the pavements are broad and made of expensive stone as though pedestrians should be treated with respect. Why does this dismay Indians after a week?

Why is it that after a short period of enjoying the rich world, they long to go back ‘home’, to a place where there is order only when viewed from the aircraft? As the plane descends, sharp edges of roads become ambiguous, geometric lines of civilization dissolving into irregular forms, and neat clusters of homes begin to look like infestations. On the roads, there is no place for the naivety of grace. If you are driving and stop for the pedestrians to cross the road, they just don’t understand why you stopped. They may even abuse you for not getting on with it. And the air, of course, is filled with small particles that would kill hundreds of thousands.

Why does the re-entry into total civic chaos comfort Indians? Even make them happy? Happy in a conditional, all-things-considered way, but still happy. What fills them with gloom in advanced economies? Tourists and longer-term visitors, even residents, say those places do not have ‘a soul’, that the people are too formal, and life is somehow lonely. Some say it just is not home. Some are more explicit and may arrive at the heart of the syndrome. They say what disturbs them about the First World is what they love for the first few days or weeks—the order, the precious rules, the straight lines of life, the formality of being, the quiet of sophistication. This is not how humans want to live, they say.

Can Indians really despise order so much? Is order an invention of white people?

My general suspicions of Indians push me to believe that they have devised romantic notions about their love for disorder to camouflage what really torments them in the First World—the collapse of street social hierarchy. They are not the elites any more. In fact, many of them suddenly become the underclass in those places. But this hypothesis is defeated by the fact that even poor Indians, when displaced, are upset by civic order.

There might be some beauty in disorder, something that appeals to an innate human idea of freedom. In nature, symmetry is the only form of order; the rest is not chaos but not order either. So maybe people see in order something unnatural, an enslavement to higher beings. Across our history, all master races created order while all the enslaved people craved to break it. An Indian stopping his car at the red light, just beyond the bloody clear white line; and a pedestrian somehow waiting for the light to turn green to cross the road—I cannot fully accept these are unintentional actions of a race without some faculty. They appear to me unconscious trial signals of a whole species that wishes to end order.

There is a difference between freedom, which is feral, and liberty, which is an invention. Liberty, despite the fanfare attending it, is restricted freedoms granted by an authority. In civic disorder, liberty collapses into illicit freedom, the formality of discipline transforms into rustic informality, which is probably endearing (until there is an accident). Mitsuhiko Yamashita, a director of Nissan Motors, once told me when I asked him how the Indian driving experience could be improved, ‘First, separate the humans, the cars, and the cattle, please.’

The more advanced an administration, the more efficiently it is able to hide its humans, and other animals. This inevitably leads to a reduction of the visible density of life. A consequence of order is always the optics of human scarcity because the people have been neatly distributed across various ways of getting some place. That’s why the first reaction of Indians in many advanced regions of the world is—‘where are the people?’ It is a question that is hard to ask in most of India. And people, despite all their grievances, like to see other people.

The disorder of India also converts the nation into a giant video game. Every moment in a public space is a navigation through unpredictable events. It is not just about how everyone drives.

Last year, my Uber driver stopped the car, took his water bottle from under his seat and went to have a dump by the roadside. I was in the car, on the shoulder of an eight-lane expressway that connects the national capital to its most affluent suburb, waiting for a man to finish his defecation. I was introduced to him by a complex algorithm funded by billions in postmodern money. I wondered if that was culture, that moment there as I was waiting for the man to finish his business. I wondered if economic progress would make such moments vanish—would it not be a destruction of something very Indian? Poverty is not only an economic condition; it is a very distinct line of human behaviour. In poverty is the heritage of us all.

Could that moment have happened in Europe? The event had two distinct parts—the man stopping on the shoulder of the expressway, and second, defecating on the wayside of an expressway. Neither of the two parts would have been possible in an affluent nation. Watching India, and watching people survive India, gives us a certain depth while a more advanced and compassionate region infantilizes its humans. Maybe Indians veer towards disorder because it saves them from a facile, dull life. An orderly, safe life, but a dull one. Maybe we unconsciously do wish to preserve disorder. We are a crooked line civilization in a world created by straight line civilizations.

So, the rich and the poor feed off Indian chaos. The rich see in the disorder the romantic vision of humanness; the poor see a world familiar to them.

But there is a place where the average Indian suddenly follows the rules. There is a place where he doesn’t even spit; though that is almost like not breathing for most Indians.

(ALMOST) none of the 5 million Indians who take the Delhi Metro every day spit or litter or urinate in the transit system until they leave the metro when they become free once again to do all this on the streets. Why do Indians behave in the Delhi Metro the way they would in an advanced foreign nation?

We can understand civic order in the Delhi Metro without the hilarious hypothesis of the conscientious Indian. When the Delhi Metro began operations in 2002, it was an extraordinary service and unprecedented in a nation that had until then viewed good design and air conditioning as a luxury for the majority. (Even the private sector had that view. Around 2010, a top executive of a call centre in Gurgaon told me: ‘The swanky office is to impress the foreign client. Some of our people who work inside, I know they would be happy in a cowshed.’)

In the beginning the poor were somewhat intimidated by the Delhi Metro. It was the first time that a space that they used, and not just gawked at, had escalators. The trains had air conditioning. And automatic doors (which would have made Act Three of some Indian films impossible.) It was all so swanky, like a mall. Yet, they were not trespassers here trying to steal cool air or glimpses of the rich. At the very beginning, I saw labourers whisper in the trains instead of speaking aloud. Now they are themselves when they travel, with much banter and laughter and even fighting, but not so comfortable that they would dirty the trains or the stations. Why?

The trains and the platforms are so well designed that commuters have very little need to violate their codes. Also, the nature of stature. Delhi Metro has stature. It is the very anti-matter of India. Trains arrive when the electronic board says they will (usually), even when the time displayed is not divisible by five or ten. Because the Delhi Metro is excellent at most things it does, commuters take seriously its threat of imposing fines for spitting and throwing garbage. Everybody also knows, except for some careless lovers in the night trains, that the Metro is watching.

But outside the Metro we are largely an ugly nation. I repeat this because it never ceases to baffle me—most countries look richer than they really are; Indian roads make India look much poorer than it is.

I live in Gurugram, one of the richest cities in India. Six feet above the ground, when no Indian is in the frame, parts of Gurugram look First World. But at the ground level, it is like any other urban mess of the nation. There are heaps of garbage on the road. Traffic signals hide behind trees, traffic cops too, waiting to pounce on some guy. Most roads do not have markers for pedestrians to cross and most Indians never really cross the road; rather, they flee death with that sheepish expression of being encroachers in their own city. And the face of the government, the police, elicit no respect, not only because they are perceived as corrupt, but also because of how they look—how they wear their uniforms, often with slippers, and how they place barricades squeezing dense traffic into zig-zag formations—India’s definition of ‘security’—and how they sit on the roadside on plastic chairs, their large paunches like babies on their laps; and how six of them hide under flyovers to pounce on motorists in the great game that is the Indian daily life. How do they find those plastic chairs? Do they bring them from home?

Usually, Indian politicians do not try to reform Indians. It is too risky. Modi was brave and self- confident to be the face of hefty fines on Indians for things they may have thought were their birthright, like disbelieving the myth of lanes, pedestrian crossings, and other posh white markings on the road. But even he did not push beyond a point. He has now given up. I feel that a society has to come naturally to a point where public order does not make its people feel as though they are encroaching into foreign culture. Indians were ready with the advent of the Delhi Metro in 2002 to endure order for about an hour of commute. But we are not there yet to endure it for many hours a day—on the roads. But what the Delhi Metro tells me is that a day can come, at some point in the future, when Indian roads are uniform, standardized, efficient, swanky, and well-designed. In their civic affluence, and repression of rules, Indians will finally concede that maybe this is not as foreign as they once thought, that this is a better way to live.

Until then, our vast aesthetic poverty will remind the average Indian that he belongs here; the nation has not left him behind.



CHAPTER 5: IF YOU DO WELL IN EXAMS, YOU WILL ESCAPE…
----------------------------------------------------



How the false hope of higher education calms the poor

In 2006, I set out to find out who exactly were the Indian nuclear scientists. I went to Mumbai’s Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC). In this sprawling establishment of India’s nuclear programme that stood on the edge of a creek, shrouded by mangroves and official secrets, there was an unmistakable touch of middle-class India. Here, security primarily meant jobs.

To the urban upper-class youth, India’s atomic technology may be a glorious taunt to the world, but the truth is that they almost never apply. Nor do the graduates from the revered Indian Institutes of Technology and the next two or three layers of Indian engineering’s top soil. For some time now, this country’s nuclear programme has been run by valiant strugglers from villages and small towns who mostly come from colleges not many have heard of.

The official of the Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) told me applications for research positions come from places like Airty, Digras, and Bareilly. Job interviews, faculty members say, can be eventful.

An applicant was once asked to explain the Uncertainty Principle, a theory which is at the heart of quantum mechanics. ‘Sir,’ he said, ‘it means everything in life is uncertain.’ When reminded of Werner Heisenberg and that the question was scientific in nature, not philosophical, the boy confessed that he had not heard of the man. Dr R. R. Puri, former head of Human Resources, remembered asking a boy about the photo-electric effect, the discovery that was a significant reason why a Nobel was awarded to Albert Einstein. The boy said, ‘You know photo. You know electric. Combine the two.’ Dr S. Datta remembered asking, ‘Why do Kolkata trams run on Direct Current and not Alternating Current?’ The applicant said, ‘If it uses Alternating Current, the tram will go forward and back.’

Some applicants told their interviewers candidly that they had come merely to see Mumbai and then asked the interviewers to please sign the travel allowance slip for ‘second-class rail travel’ from their home towns, a new improvement from an earlier offer to pay ‘the lowest fare by the shortest route’.

Denied the easy talent that lands at the doorsteps of other employers, BARC tries hard to find a gem. Interviews here are long patient hearings and last for about three months. With most of the applicants not fluent in English or even Hindi, a professor dining in the canteen mess sometimes gets an emergency call to arrive at the interview session because he is the only one who understands, say, Malayalam.

Interviews turn melancholic too, when some rejected applicants cry and plead, ‘Ask me one more question, sir, just one more.’ There are long spells during the interviews when ‘the future of India’s nuclear programme seems to be bleak,’ as Dr R. B. Grover, founder and former director of Homi Bhabha National Institute told me. ‘But then a bright chap comes along and the mood swings to sheer joy and great hopes for the future.’

I met some students on campus. Akshat Kakkar, in a blue shirt and pink tie, was from Bareilly in Uttar Pradesh. Every time he went back home, his neighbours asked, ‘Have you seen a nuclear bomb? How big is it? Have you touched it?’ An uncle, in fact, thought that Bombay got its name because it housed an atomic bomb.

Ashish Dike, son of a cotton farmer in Vidarbha’s Kalgaon village, finds it hard to believe that there are people in the world who object to nuclear technology. ‘In some villages in the Satara district, people think that windmills drive away the rains. People who are against clean energy like nuclear power are as ignorant.’ Radiation, that much maligned thing in the outside world, is almost loved at BARC. ‘Without radiation, species will not mutate,’ a senior scientist whispered to me. ‘There will be no evolution.’

They do not have to sell this hard to India’s villages, where there is great pride around the Indian nuclear programme. ‘There is so much patriotism in small towns,’ Professor Grover told me. ‘It’s they who really take pride in the nation.’ And it is from there that most of the students come today. Bhabha Atomic Research Centre, a senior faculty member told me, ‘is run by villages’.

But what draws those students is not patriotism. They may have a lot of it, and they may even say that is why they were there for the interviews. But higher education in science is a form of hope India extends to its poor and lower-middle classes. It was a hope that once defined my generation, when we were the new Indian middle class.

When I was growing up in Madras, the boys were fated to prepare for one engineering entrance exam. I recorded these memories of one of the Brahmin colonies where I was raised, in my second novel, The Illicit Happiness of Other People:


    The day they were born and were diagnosed with having a penis, their fate was decided—to one day take the Joint Entrance Exam. The toughest exam in the world, their fathers say. One in a hundred would make it. Their fathers tell them every day of their lives, sometimes holding a leather belt in their hands, that the JEE alone will decide their lives because it will eventually take them to America on a full scholarship. On a street where every boy knows that his future rests on a single test with multiple-choice questions, it is appropriate that the four identical buildings here are named A, B, C and D.

The IITs were never great centres of learning by world standards. Rather, they were museums that collected young Indians with excellent quantitative abilities. For decades, until recently, the IITians were from urban, literate middle-class families, and it was inevitable that their success would inspire small-town and rural Indians to prepare for the mother of all entrance exams. As a result, a new kind of IITian has been entering those institutes. They are not extraordinary quizzers or musicians or poets, which the IITians of my generation were rumoured to be. The new IITians are trained to crush an exam. They were told that was what they had to do to liberate themselves from their bleak lives, and they worked in inhuman ways to achieve that. But they are not bright in other ways, in ways the generations before them were—that is, bright in an urbane, globalized, anglicized way.

For some time now, IIT professors and alumni have been mourning the falling quality of the students. Once, Narayana Murthy, the co-founder of Infosys and an IIT alumnus, told an audience in New York that the new IITians were substandard. ‘They somehow get through the Joint Entrance Examination. But their performance in IITs, at jobs or when they come for higher education in institutes in the US is not as good as it used to be.’

This is a classic case of co-option: The elite promote a way of life in which they are highly adept as the best way to be; in effect, they tell others what the elite are actually always saying without realizing it: ‘why can’t you be like us’; the poor then do everything they can to imitate the elite but they fall short because there are aspects of living that cannot be imitated, aspects of parenting and cultural marination that cannot be aped; the poor then become grotesque versions of their elite; the elite then migrate or are displaced from their strongholds, and move on to what is considered prestigious by people they discreetly consider superior to them (like white people), and their former strongholds, which they have vacated, are now filled by the lower-middle class and the poor, diminishing the lure of the very things that were once prestigious. Among the modern cultural secrets of India is the feeling many urban middle-class Indians get when they visit their prestigious colleges today—those vaunted institutes are today occupied by provincial students who look poor and ‘downmarket’, who speak in their mother tongues and not English. This pursuit of higher education that promises to convert millions into clerks plays a part in taming the disenchantments of those who were not lucky enough to be born in the right homes. This was how the previous generations of the almost-poor were tamed. In fact, this was how all of the Indian middle class was tamed by the British. It was not a collateral effect of some colonial plan. Instead, that was the plan, it appears—to tame Indians by making them clerks.

MANY Indians believe that their first prime minister, Jawaharlal Nehru, was the man who began the process of creating a vast new educated, salaried middle-class that would dedicate their entire life to first acquiring knowledge they did not want, then working in mind- numbing jobs serving as clerks in corporations and governments, and ensuring that their children, too, became clerks. But Nehru himself may have been co- opted by the British in this matter, just as they did through socialism and other liberal religions of his time.

In The Man Who Knew Infinity, a biography of the Indian mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan by Robert Kanigel, I was surprised to come across an extract from an analysis published in 1907 called, ‘Report on Canara, Malabar and Ceded Districts’. It was by a British bureaucrat called William Thackeray (not the more famous writer).


    The report said: It is very proper that in England, a good share of the produce of the earth should be appropriated to support certain families in affluence, to produce senators, sages and heroes… The leisure, independence and high ideals which the enjoyment of this rent affords has enabled them to raise Britain to pinnacles of glory. Long may they enjoy it. But in India that haughty spirit, independence and deep thought which the possession of great wealth sometimes gives ought to be suppressed. They are directly averse to our power and interest. The nature of things, the past experience of all governments, renders it unnecessary to enlarge on this subject. We do not want generals, statesmen and legislators; we want industrious husbandmen.

(There is one more line that Kanigel has quoted which is inessential to conveying the idea of the diabolic plan. But as I am a Malayalee, I am culturally bound to advertise what at least some colonizers may have thought of us then. After Thackeray says that it is in the interest of the English not to raise great intellects and thinkers from among Indians he adds, ‘If we wanted restless and ambitious spirits there are enough of them in Malabar to supply the whole peninsula.’)

This ‘report’ sounds exactly like one of those periodical hoaxes that Indian patriots circulate on social media. I do wonder if Kanigel had been fooled. Kanigel has a big reputation as a journalistic writer but I cannot shake off my suspicion that he was conned by an early Hindu nationalist, possibly a Malayalee. The bibliography of The Man Who Knew Infinity states that he sourced Thackeray’s report from A History of South India by Pran Nath Chopra et al. So it is a secondary reference, Kanigel himself has not seen the original document. But I do not dispute that the British would have preferred a colony of clerks instead of free-range men who had ideas. The British did manage to convert the middle class into ‘industrious husbandmen’.

Nehru then went on to invest an impoverished nation’s meagre resources in higher education for the elite instead of going for universal primary education. (Jean Drèze and Amartya Sen, in their book, An Uncertain Glory, use this fact to argue that newly independent India was not ‘socialist’ as most Indians think, because socialist nations, for all their faults, invested heavily in primary education, health of women and children, and other aspects of equality, something that Kerala alone did.)

All of the modern Indian middle class is a creation of a wilful or unintended subjugation of intellectual spirit. Layers and layers of social and economic classes that strive to rise are, and will be, subsumed by a civilization-sized pressure to ‘study’ for the dullest reason why humans ever choose to study—to receive a salary.

So many began to pursue the path to public and private clerkdom that India had to filter out most applicants. So, in time, India became a nation of super tough entrance exams. They were tough because very few would be chosen. Many were aptitude tests that derived their format from one of the most discredited aspects of gauging human intelligence—intelligence quotient tests.

Venkatraman Ramakrishnan, who was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2009, told me in the course of an interview: ‘Now we define intelligence in this narrow way, by way of IQ tests. IQ tests were originally designed to detect mental retardation, they were never designed to detect genius. So they are being slightly misused. Well, not slightly, more than slightly misused. ’

This means that a very low score points to mental retardation and a high score says nothing about a person’s intellect. Even so, various types of aptitude tests are the very lifelines for the poorest students to a better life. Most of them are not culturally suited for this sort of evaluation because these tests need some exposure to the West, or at least a Western system of schooling. So one man devised an IQ test for Indian tribals to see what would happen. Some of the children scored so well that they qualified to join Mensa, a global community for those whose IQs are considered to be in the top 2 per cent of the general population. THE discovery of high IQ among tribal children is a consequence of an experiment conducted by the ecologist Narayan Desai. Originally, Desai was concerned about the preservation of forests and their environment. He realized that woke and urban interest in ecology would be less effective in protecting the environment than the engagement of the impoverished people who live off those lands. He decided to seek out the very ‘smartest’ among them, whom he could then groom. (He, naturally, believes there is a correlation between high IQ and smartness.) Desai, who is a member of Mensa himself, decided that he would use the very IQ tests to choose the gifted from pools of ten- to fifteen-year-olds who were fortunate enough to go to the free schools in their localities run by the government or charities.

The Mensa tests differ from region to region to neutralize the effects of culture. But all versions must be cleared by Mensa International. The tests that Desai administers to India’s poorest children ensure that the limitations of their formal education do not interfere with the evaluation of their mental abilities. He declined a request for sample questions because, he said, Mensa International does not allow him to share that information. From his hints, though, the type of question that would certainly not be a part of the tribal Mensa test is, ‘What’s the anagram of Britney Spears?’ A more likely question is to identify geometric patterns in a given diagram.

In 2002, he tested about 200 students. Four— all girls—scored high enough to qualify for Mensa. Over the years, Desai and his associates have screened thousands and have seen hundreds qualify for Mensa, who are then offered financial support by non- governmental organizations and individuals. They will be groomed to become exam-takers, and have a stake in a peaceful stable nation run by clerks. It is their best chance to escape the hustle of microbusiness.

What about the poor who don’t score high in aptitude tests? Generally, they are persuaded to sit perplexed in humanities courses conceptualized in Europe.

IN August 2008, the Indian edition of Vogue ran a fashion spread that featured poor Indians holding expensive objects, including a Burberry umbrella and a Hermès Birkin bag. Outrage erupted across India— chiefly from the Zara constituency, where most of the righteous exist, but also from the Hermès crowd. Ten years later, they may have seen another image of a poor Indian holding a luxury item but they were not offended; in fact, they loved it. It was a photograph of thirty-one-year-old Kanhaiya Kumar standing with his mother, holding a bound copy of his ‘African Studies’ doctoral thesis.

At first glance, it is a happy photograph. He was submitting his thesis to his mother who, he tweeted, ‘never let poverty interfere with my dreams’.

Embedded in this happy photograph is also a sad image if we know how to frame it. A bright young man from a poor family, who had no interest in science or mathematics, was compelled by the pervading elite influence to invest the best years of his life in higher education in the luxury streams of humanities. He first graduated in geography, and devoted his twenties to a doctoral trope in Jawaharlal Nehru University, the refuge of arts students who want to escape adult life. What Nehruvians call the aspirations of the poor are in reality co-option by the rich. The poor, especially the smart, are always co-opted into systems where the rich hold all the cards. It is cruelly funny that Kanhaiya’s thesis must be titled ‘The Process of Decolonization and Social Transformation in South Africa’. For what he had surrendered to, by pursuing the mythical prestige of a doctoral thesis, was a form of colonization in itself.

It would be unspeakably foolish to think that knowledge is meant for the rich alone. The poor who crave to accumulate knowledge, to understand, say, the political history of a distant African nation, are lucky. But if they are doing it just to get a degree, respectability, or prolonged hostel accommodation, then it is a very expensive mistake.

But then isn’t it true that Kanhaiya Kumar has succeeded? He is a voice of conscience. Considering his personality, if it were not for the long asylum of JNU and its student politics, he may have become a nameless journalist in the Hindi media who may have been co-opted by activism, which is journalism at a 50 per cent discount. And what is so great about becoming a call centre employee or another kind of a corporate slave? Instead, today he is something in politics. He says humane things in Hindi, a language that has in recent times become a medium of aggression and hatred. And he says it all like a stand-up comedian. He is a comedian who is not called a comedian, a writer who is not considered a writer, a theatre actor who is not known as an actor. People underestimate how powerful it is to be what the world does not think you are. He is also a politician who is not fully identified as a politician. All this happened because he surrendered to the co-option of the old elites in the humanities.

But then the accident of success, the accident of a foolish move by the Modi government to send cops after him, that converted him into a martyr and brought national attention to his exquisite oratorical skills, masks a bleak truth. There are millions of smart young men like him who don’t make it. Without the lottery of success, the lives of humanities students from poor families are fragile.

Rohith Vemula is an example of a boy who did not make it. Even though he wished to be a science writer, he abandoned microbiology for sociology—that nest of melancholic left-wing activism. He did not realize one thing about the posh leftists, whom he tried to imitate or emulate. They transmit a lot of negativity but they go back to their aesthetic spaces. Boys like Vemula return to their depressing hostel rooms with their smell of Margo soap, damp walls, and sweaty lungis. Activism is an extremely dangerous space for the depressed among the poor. In the end, Vemula killed himself. That was when he found success. He became a household name in India, for a month, when his death was exploited by activists who attributed to it, as they do with suicides, the reasons that served them best.

Even Kanhaiya almost did not make it. Until he was saved by politics, real politics. That is what is common between him and his arch-enemy—Narendra Modi. Both of them are poor men, or lower-middle class by Indian standards if you want to be strict, who would have amounted to nothing if it were not for politics. India is rigged in such a way that apart from sports, the only other sphere where the talented poor can do well is politics.

But then even as a politician Kanhaiya has been co-opted by old money. For a sanctimonious man who has disdain for capitalism, he was at first a part of one of the most corrupt and murderous organizations in India —the Communist Party of India, a farcical derivative of some thought experiments of two wealthy German migrants taken too seriously.

I have no quarrel with the poor viewing higher education in the humanities as a place to park their lives until they figure out how to survive the new world. My worry is that they are beginning to see through the farce. And they are beginning to express their rage at being stranded through self-destructive political activism. There is something specific I have to tell them, about why much of higher education is a farce—as a former poor student to the modern poor student.

IN 2019, a protest broke out among students who objected to a fee hike in Jawaharlal Nehru University. Most of them had spent thousands of rupees in various fees to collect a degree. In essence, their fight against the government was to pay half the ransom and not the whole ransom. They should instead have fought to pay nothing at all. What India’s young should be fighting for is the abolition of higher education as a measure of a person’s worth in the job market.

By the time I entered high school, my family had slipped from lower-middle class to absurdly poor. I was poor in the sense that even Mother Teresa would have called me poor. At sixteen, when I entered college, I chose English literature. (I was a year younger than the rest of my batch due to a clerical oversight and not precocity.) I wished to become a writer, but I was not stupid enough to think a literature course would help. I chose literature for a dishonourable reason.

The administration of Loyola College was confused because my highest marks in the board exams were in math and computer science, yet I wanted to enter a stream that had by then become a refuge for boys with learning disabilities, the blind, and those who were on their way to becoming Catholic priests. I could not tell the college that my reason was that I would have to work part-time to feed myself and pay the fees too, probably, and I could bullshit my way to a literature degree, a feat I was not confident of pulling off in math or the sciences. Eventually, I did get by without ever seriously studying Chaucer or Shakespeare, about whom I wrote many impressive essays in exams. Now and then, I sold Primus Kabsons stoves and Eureka Forbes products in showrooms, and Funskool toys door to door, and milked college festivals for speaking and writing cash prizes. I did not attend my convocation because I did not consider it worth the hard-earned ₹200 I had to pay to attend.

There were thousands like me who were harmed by the religion of higher education. I should not have been forced to pursue a piece of laminated paper. At sixteen, I should have been free to work for a newspaper or a film company, instead of wasting three years listening to provincial professors who tried to teach me the greatness of some British writers who would have been in unremarkable graves if their country’s brutal merchants had not colonized the world.

Oh yes, education is about acquiring knowledge. That’s wonderful for those who really want that.

The most humane reform modern India can make is to ensure that a college degree is not a qualification for non-technical or non-scientific jobs, or even for any aptitude test, including the civil services exam. A written test and a series of job interviews are not a perfect assessment of a human, but still a far superior form of ascertaining a person’s abilities than a piece of paper about him. In fact, for jobs that do not require preloaded technical knowledge, it should be made illegal for corporations and other organizations to demand a college degree, because a degree is a form of economic and social discrimination.

Many noted Indians did not complete college and they have fabricated college degrees. Politicians and other luminaries who have emerged from poverty should, instead of fabricating degree certificates, campaign to end the farce. This will liberate millions of youth; suddenly the formal job market in the private and public sectors will be open to a class of Indians who are smart but did not buy membership to a club that is controlled by the elite.

A few years ago, Japan contemplated ending humanities courses altogether. I don’t believe India should ever do that. If we set aside the rubbish of degrees, there is value in a formal arts and philosophy education. My complaint is only against the veneration of higher education.

As part of the JNU protests, a group of Dalit students claimed that higher education is their revolution against ‘Brahmin hegemony’. They were humanities students. How I laughed. What they probably meant was that they were trying to defeat the local thug by paying protection money to a bigger thug—the West. But their higher education is, in fact, their co-option by this ‘Brahmin hegemony’.

All revolutions against the elite that involve imitating the elite, fail. You don’t fight Brahmins by glorifying what they have created; rather, by destroying it. As a literature student, what I learned is that half of humanities studies are a veneration of a wrong analysis of the world by wealthy white men. Some of whom were probably mentally ill. One of the fathers of sociology is Auguste Comte, who invented and wore attire that had buttons only in the back so that he would not be able to wear it without assistance, without labour, without creating a job. Thus, he could create a community even in the simple act of dressing up.

Also, the poor are conned by prestige. The nature of prestige is such that it ceases to exist when the poor demonstrate that they can achieve it. That is why the refined affluent who express their concern for the poor students of JNU do not send their own children there. Instead, they will buy them an unsurpassable education in the US. Shouldn’t everyone who believes in equality deny their own children a huge head start? There is a moment in the TV series Silicon Valley when a character loosely based on the venture capitalist Peter Thiel is giving a talk.

Tech VC: College has become a cruel expensive joke on the poor and the middle class that benefits only the perpetrators of it.

Ex-hippie in the audience: You are a dangerous man, spewing ignorance.

VC: I’m just saying people should trust themselves more than a system that happily churns out unemployed debtors and provides dubious value.


    Hippie: The true value of a college education is intangible. VC: The true value of snake-oil is intangible as well. Hippie: Fascist.

INDIANS can see this clearly, but they cannot get rid of the stigma of not possessing a college degree. Prime Minister Narendra Modi himself has been accused of faking two college degrees. The way people have scorned Modi for the probability that he never went to college, it is as though they possessed some great intelligence or skill to walk into college. It is particularly amusing when those who scorn are graduates in the humanities. It takes little effort to get an arts degree. Almost all Indians got into college because they filled an application form and their parents paid their entire fees. And most emerged from college with no special skill or knowledge. You may argue that the issue is not whether college matters, but in the deception. But any Indian faking an arts degree is only responding to an evil force. The prestige attached to college degrees is a caste system. All educated Indians are complicit in this. The hope contained in a college degree has faded for the poor. As a result, in the future its pursuit will cease to be among the factors that maintain the peace of the classes.

Indian politicians have begun to address this. In July 2022, Modi gave a speech to educators who had assembled to discuss the implementation of the National Educational Policy. He said that modern India’s education system ‘emerged from slavery’. And that its objective was petty. ‘The British, in their self- interest, set up a system of education that was meant to incubate a servant class.’ He said very little has changed since the colonizers left. And that it was time India created more useful people than ‘degree-holders’.

I AM not entirely against the false hope of education. Because I feel most of hope is false and that is all right. (In fact, when people say, ‘believe in yourself’ they mean, ‘be delusional’. But it works.) But the time of education’s false hope is up. It is not fooling the poor any more, but at the same time, they do not have anything better to latch on to. We need to replace it with some other aspiration.



CHAPTER 6: THE POOR ARE THE WORST ENEMIES OF THE POOR
-----------------------------------------------------

THE general perception of envy is that it is a reaction of the deprived to those who have everything. Even India’s most famous intellectual, Chanakya, had this view: ‘only lazy and poor people’ develop envy, and that ‘prostitutes envy loyal women, and widows envy married women’. This is a wrong analysis of human nature. Envy is a thing between equals.

Whom do you envy? Not whom you think you should envy, but whom do you actually envy? Chances are you don’t envy Elon Musk or Mukesh Ambani, or any other billionaire (assuming you are not one yourself, in which case you would be envying other billionaires). You are more likely to envy your neighbours, colleagues, cousins, siblings, or spouse, or all of them. Chanakya had got this totally wrong. A prostitute is more likely to be envious of a more beautiful prostitute than ‘a loyal woman’ who is not in the trade.

The Greek poet Hesiod agrees with me in Works and Days (in a translation by Hugh G. Evelyn-White) ‘...potter is angry with potter, and craftsman with craftsman, and beggar is jealous of beggar, and minstrel of minstrel.’

(That is why, in your grander private moments, what may annoy you about those who envy you is not that they defame and slander, but that those bastards really do think they are your equals.)

There is another powerful force that equality exerts, which is derived from the same complex strands of people sharing the same social plane: hatred. Equals compete for the same band of resources. As in the case of envy, the poor may have a theoretical sense of whom they should hate more—‘the big people’. But whom they actually hate is very different—people who are very much like them.

Let me present what I see on most days in Gurugram. Housemaids are stopped at the gates of a colony and harassed by security guards. Also, harassment is a form of flirtation. Usually, the only threats the maids face during their journey from their tenements to their affluent places of work are from men and women who are a lot like them. Traffic cops harass motorcyclists more often than owners of luxury sedans. All the bribes that the poor pay are to people from their own class or an economic rung not much higher than their own. The violent war between police and Naxals is in reality a war between the poor and the poorer. The rich do harm the poor in invisible and complex ways but the most visibly violent and unjust experiences of the nation are within various sections of the poor. Like caste atrocities.

The Dalits, who are among the poorest Indians, should have the clearest view of the elite as foes. But they face most of their visible everyday injustice from their own.

‘Dalits’ is a term we Indians use to refer to the ‘lowest caste’, but this is erroneous. The Dalits were the original ‘outcastes’. Many people think ‘outcaste’ is a figurative expression, which it is, of course. But ‘outcaste’ also means exactly what the word says.

In general Indian consciousness, the four castes were defined by their historical professions. Brahmins were the priestly intellectual class; the Kshatriyas were the ruling or administrative class; the Vaishyas were the merchant class; and the Shudras were the labour class. Most Indians think that the ‘Shudras’ were the ‘untouchables’. To the extent we know history, this is not true. Shudras, who were ranked the ‘lowest’ of the four castes, were traditionally farmers, traders, and craftsmen, apart from being farmhands and servants. They are not the ‘Dalits’. They are today widely known as Other Backward Classes (OBCs); the caste identity of Prime Minister Modi, and the very core of the Indian ruling class today. But there was a whole population of Indians who were outside the caste structure. They were not allowed to live inside the mainstream villages. They were, truly, the ‘outcastes’. Today they come under ‘Scheduled Castes’ and ‘Scheduled Tribes’. Even the Shudras treated them as though they were not human.

In any given situation in this country, someone is ‘high’ and someone is ‘low’. This is true even within the Dalits. There are about a thousand recognized subcastes within the Dalit community that comprises over 200 million people, and they follow a caste hierarchy among themselves. The top echelons of the community, like the Vankars and Mahars, do not dine with or marry, or even touch the lowest layers like the Valmikis, who have traditionally been sanitation workers. Once, when Martin Macwan, a Dalit human rights activist, ate in a Valmiki household in a Gujarat village, he was denied entry into a Vankar village for that reason. Ranjana, a Dalit who worked as a maid in Mumbai told me, ‘Particularly in the villages, even our community does not mingle with Valmikis.’ Thus, Dalits perpetrate the same crimes that they accuse the upper castes of committing. This is inspiring the bottom rungs of Dalits, the untouchables among the untouchables, to ask for reservations within the Scheduled Caste quota. Dalit leaders have always feared a looming civil war within the community—a reason why they don’t like talking about it very much.

Many at the bottom of the Dalit pyramid move to the cities to seek anonymity and escape discrimination. In the cities, the ‘lowest of the low’ Dalits settle for professions that should not exist as an option for human beings. They are hired by morgues, especially in Ahmedabad, to squat on corpses during autopsies and take out body parts to show the doctor who may not want to touch the cadaver. They clean human excreta with their hands, including in Jain monasteries where the meditating inmates refuse to install flushes in the fear that flushing will kill germs, something that is unacceptable to their faith. Valmikis also get into manholes where they die sometimes after inhaling poisonous gases.

These lowest subcastes of the Dalit community that go by different names in different states of India have always complained that the elite Dalits are the ones who have enjoyed the quotas the most. Communities like Chamars in Maharashtra and Malas in Andhra Pradesh have over the years prospered because they had major social and economic head starts, which made them unbeatable in the battle for reserved seats in colleges and in reserved government jobs, which deepened the inequality between them and the rest. This, while communities like Musahars still catch rats for a living.

As a result of the growing financial and even social inequality between various Dalit subcastes, the demands of the lowest groups to further fragment the Scheduled Caste reservation are growing stronger, but are muffled by the fact that influential Dalit leaders are against such a liquidation of their political heft. ‘Splitting the country into any more caste categories can disintegrate the nation,’ the Dalit leader Udit Raj told me a few years ago.

By 2008, in Andhra Pradesh, Haryana, and to some extent in Karnataka, the movement to further split the SC quota was formidable. The Chandrababu Naidu government had, in fact, divided the Dalit job reservation in Andhra Pradesh into four quotas, each inaccessible to the other. The Supreme Court struck this down as unconstitutional. In Haryana, the Dalit community is divided into A and B.

In Andhra Pradesh, Naxalites of the People’s War Group incited the exceptionally impoverished subcaste called Madigas to carve out a separate quota. G. Shankar, secretary general of A. P. Scheduled Caste Welfare Association, told me, ‘Such splintering is very complicated, because one subcaste which is not doing well in one state may be doing well in another.’

Resources are finite, life is hard, and equals are rivals. This is also one of the reasons why the poorest Hindus hate the poorest Muslims. They are economic rivals. So the Hindu poor, though they dislike the ruling classes, vote for Hindu nationalism because it subdues one major segment of their competitors. Violence against migrants in India is often couched in a cultural war but it is almost always over jobs and other meagre finite resources.

IN December 2019, India enacted the Citizenship (Amendment) Act. It provided a special way for persecuted religious minorities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan who had arrived in India before 2014 to become Indian citizens, as long as they were not Muslims. It was the first time India used religion as a criterion for citizenship. There were massive protests because millions of Muslims who lived in India with no proper documentation worried that the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party was trying to make them vanish somehow.

Usually, societies that wish to discriminate against a religion take great pains to cloak the act in respectable economic and humanitarian terms. The Indian government, on the other hand, had taken great pains to present its reasonable economic and humanitarian intentions as religious bias.

India’s economic intent is to favour poor Indians over poor foreigners. (Muslim immigrants compete directly with poor Indians.) India’s humanitarian intent is to grant citizenship to those who have fled theocracies in the neighbourhood and are so few that they cannot alter Indian politics or exert a strain on the nation’s resources. Illegal Muslim immigrants may number in millions, and it was not practical for India to grant them citizenship. But India articulated this in a way even Islamic nations don’t. It bluntly said foreign Muslims will not be given Indian citizenship. The reactions to India’s policy were powerful and emotional. Some Hindus behaved as though they were so full of insatiable love for each other that they now needed to love ‘persecuted’ Hindus from Pakistan. And India’s suave liberals demonstrated great love for Muslim immigrants whom they absorb as servants with no fair contracts, pay poorly for twelve-hour shifts with no weekly offs, and sack at five-minutes notice.

Compassion for poor immigrants is a high virtue and emerges from mankind’s most intelligent invention—the idea that the strong should take care of the weak. But people who do not have to make any sacrifices in exchange for professing such compassion are not the finest analysts of the issue.

Across the world, the poor and the lower-middle classes despise poor migrants (for convenience I will use the word ‘migrants’ to refer to ‘immigrants’ too) because poor migrants first compete for resources and jobs with poor residents and not editorial writers and development economists.

A central quality of an immigrant is that he is competition, or perceived as competition. If you are not in competition with a migrant, you are analysing the wrong migrant. Find the migrant who is your rival and you will suddenly realize you are not so noble any more.

Consider the provincial students who are now taking over engineering colleges and against whom the old urban elite cannot compete in entrance exams. Listen carefully to how the refined condemn the new elite engineering students—as merely exam-crackers, who have no other talent, who can’t speak English, who don’t read novels, who don’t quiz, or play Carnatic music. Have you gone back to your alma mater recently, and covertly found the new students there shockingly ‘downmarket’ and did you instead express your thought as a noble concern for how ‘the standards have fallen’? These are all sophisticated ways of hating ‘the migrant’, the poor outsider, who is encroaching on your turf, your culture, your way of life, and your resources and the resources of your children.

(Also, the new type of politicians and scholars who have taken over Lutyens’ Delhi—they are the new spatial and cultural migrants. This is the very reason why conscientious economists and other humanities professors who claim to have compassion for immigrants hate the new rustic, nationalistic, and vernacular academics.)

The hatred for the potent migrant is very severe among the poor locals of a region because they undercut their wages, bring in more of their own, and form a tight community that makes them an even more formidable job market. And, of course, the migrant is usually a bit poorer than the local poor.



CHAPTER 7: THE DARK FORCE THAT GUARDS US
----------------------------------------

In the feature film version of this book, a man gets obsessed with the question, ‘why don’t the poor kill us?’ The absurdity of why the poor don’t invade and destroy his affluent colony confuses him to the point that it begins to ruin his quiet life. Producer Arya Menon sent a pitch to the actor Saif Ali Khan’s manager. A few weeks later, the actor was stabbed by a poor migrant inside his own home, as though the poor wished to refute my theories.

After the stabbing incident, the Indian middle classes had an obvious question. If a film star can be stabbed by an intruder in his home, that too in a luxury apartment in one of the safest suburbs of one of the safest cities in the country, what does it say about our safety and that of those whom we love? There is a reassuring answer, but it comes from a dark place. When Khan was in hospital, there was a familiar sight outside. His family and friends emerged from cars. There was a glow about them, and some of them wore clothes that didn’t look like clothes. They walked through a parted crowd of onlookers who looked like the rest of India, more provincial, and with no glow. Like Saif Ali Khan, many of us live in secure buildings, with cameras, some of which may also work. In the care of guards who were probably farmhands not long ago. They do protect us, but our primary protection, the thing that really guards us, is something else, something ancient maybe. It is not exactly noble, which is also why it works.

The attack on Khan was an aberration. Generally, India is safe for its rich and the middle class. Safe as in safe from criminal danger. You could end up dead on the road because of India’s road design or how Indians drive, or lose your life to lax enforcement of safety protocols in buildings, but we do not usually need to worry about criminals. In some aspects, we are safe not only compared to middle-income countries in Africa and Latin America, but also in comparison with the West.

This is so even though India’s rich live in a way that is risky. They depend on people rather than systems. Home security here is more a man on a stool than hard tech. Also, we are served by a whole pall of maids, cooks, gardeners, and drivers; we live with strangers in our homes, who have access to its every inch, who have their own lives that are unknown to us, their own mobile phones, and their own tumults, and friends with whom they surely discuss us. Even if the house help is of sterling character, we have no idea about her boyfriend, or brother, or someone she met on Instagram. My neighbour’s maid, it turned out, was in love with a mystery man from Pakistan to whom she was giving the details of the house. Most colonies require house help to clear ‘police clearance’, which is a farcical procedure. A police verification can be procured for ₹500. Yet, we are safe, we live with some reassurance from India that our families are safe. We face more danger from our peers and spouses than from India’s poor. And at some subterranean level of the mind, all of us are aware of the force that protects us.

What protects us, to the extent that it can, is the brutal side of India. If at all there is anything worse than poverty, it is an Indian jail and the Indian judicial system. It is a deadly deterrent even for criminals. India exacts a very heavy price for hurting its upper classes. Even Mumbai’s organized crime, which extorted the city’s rich, had to go.

Gangsters flourished in the 90s when they figured how to game the judicial system. When they were apprehended by the police, their lawyers swiftly got them bail. Even if they were jailed, they would run their operations from the prison. So, a section of the police decided to kill them, at least those who were useless to them.

Organized extortion ended in Mumbai because of a suspension of human rights for those the cops marked as criminals. It was generally a popular move, and greatly encouraged by the rich. In the late 90s and the early 2000s, the fear of the underworld was so severe in Mumbai that people refrained from buying luxury cars. In an article, when I mentioned the fee of a famous ‘character actor’ for his role in a Hollywood film, he called to rebuke me for being ‘irresponsible’. He was not worried about the income tax department but ‘the situation’, which meant organized crime. Everyone dreaded that call from them. The daughter of a businessman who was once abducted by the underworld, and who had to pay a lot of money in ransom for his life, told me about her vehement joy at the murders of gangsters and how much she despised films that glorified them. Eventually, the cops started eliminating many inconvenient people and claiming that they were killed in ‘encounters’. ‘Encounter’ became a Hindi word for state murder. Even gangs started killing their rivals through the cops. The whole system cooperated with the police—down to the doctor in the morgue who would interpret a point-blank shot in the head in a convenient way. A gang of cops rose in fame, and they competed to record the highest number of kills. Some of them killed more than 100; at least one is believed to have killed more than 300, an idea probably promoted by himself off the record.

(In the hundreds of underworld encounters, miraculously, no cop was killed. They were probably very good. But the only time we knew for certain that the other side, too, was armed was in November 2008 when Pakistani terrorists attacked Mumbai. A famed ‘encounter’ cop went to fight and was killed immediately in the encounter.)

Across the country, cops eliminate criminals in ‘encounters’. It is not even news any more. (In July 2022, Scroll.in stated that in the preceding six years there were 813 ‘encounter’ killings in India.) The brutality, or its potential, raises the stakes for all criminals. Not just in cities, but even in dangerous places, there appears to be a line that criminals should not cross for they would be eliminated if they did. Criminals who have killed police or are a threat to politicians and the very rich are among the doomed. Also, people who have brutalized children and are on the run are very likely to be exterminated. A crime reporter in Haryana told me, criminals who have too many cases against them, who are too broke to feed the cops, who are thus only a procedural nuisance to be ferried between jail, hospital, and court, are bumped off.

This was also how India fought terrorism, and that official armed rebellion of the poor against the state—Naxalism.

The killings are not the worst thing that the cops can do to a person. The poor who are caught face torture. Many rot away in hellish jails without a trial for years.

What India cannot do with competent legal systems, it accomplishes in informal ways. But this way of keeping us safe has a flaw. It is a flaw that is present in all security systems, and not just the criminality of police. The flaw is that its primary use to us is as a deterrent, and a deterrent is only useful against rational criminals. And there are people who are not rational.

They might be mentally disturbed, or they might be desperate drug addicts who do not think rationally. Often, the guy who manages to breach security and walk into a building does so in the most irrational way. He just walks in. Most of the time, irrational criminals are apprehended, but it is of little consequence to the victims.

Even in this regard, India’s roughness protects the upper class. Indian life is a natural filtering process. People who are clinically dangerous, especially if they are poor, do not survive too long. They are killed by the community. Or, they just die alone of neglect and disease. In comparison, more just societies like the United States and Europe appear to have more empowered dangerous people.

In December 2024, a man just walked into a New York subway train and burned a woman alive, a woman he had never known. A few days after that in New York, a man pushed another onto the subway tracks. It’s not as though these things cannot happen here, but for a nation of this size and circumstance, Indian cities appear to witness far less mindless crimes. But I wonder how long Indian cities can maintain their relative safety in a changing world where there is more compassion for the poor and the unstable, and the dangerous among them can survive far longer than before. What will happen when India finds it hard to be as brutal as before in those unseen rooms where parallel justice is dealt?



CHAPTER 8: THE DEFEAT OF THE AMATEUR INDIAN, AND HIS WORLD FRAMED IN ENGLISH
----------------------------------------------------------------------------

ONCE, the union home minister Amit Shah, who many South Indians think is a North Indian, said that India needs a unifying language, and that the language is Hindi. He immediately unified half of India, for that is all it takes to make South Indians pretend that they love each other and that they are all the same. Then Tamil politicians, who are actually actors, and Tamil actors, who are actually politicians, lamented ‘the imposition’—an English word that is extremely difficult for most Tamils to pronounce.

The fact is, no one has tried to impose Hindi on the South. But I have no doubt that the nationalistic BJP government in Delhi dreams of an India where there is a single dominant language which, coincidentally, they speak well. After all, there can be no nationalism without a national language. As things stand, there is no such thing as an Indian nationalist; there are only the North Indian nationalists. The exceptions are, oddly, the English-speaking new patriots.

In India, ‘national’ tends to mean a thing that is not national. Like the national animal (tiger), national bird (peacock), national sport (hockey), and national capital (Delhi). National is often someone’s regional stuff, just like ‘global culture’ is the local culture of white people. The South has accepted many alien things as ‘national’, but it is too late for them to accept Hindi. The people who want Hindi to be the national language wish that the South agrees to be colonized by the language. Then one half of India will not be so foreign to the other half, and a nationalistic Indian prime minister would not have to, absurdly, speak to the poorest Tamils or patriotic space scientists in English. But then, people do not surrender to a language to be ‘unified’. People learn languages that are useful. What is the use of Hindi? What are the rewards of learning Hindi? Even parents are not very appealing if they are not useful. Hindi cannot colonize the South because Hindi is useless.

Guess who surrendered to Hindi when it appeared to have a certain allure? Rajinikanth. He was willing to act as the hero’s sidekick in Hindi films so that he could have a shot at Mumbai’s film industry, which was at the time more lucrative than Tamil. So did the great Kamal Haasan, another Tamil superstar. A language does not conquer through war; it slowly encroaches through rewards. Rajinikanth and Kamal Haasan, who failed in their efforts to become Hindi stars, today condemn Hindi’s attempt to trespass into their fiefdoms. This is because that language was never useful to them.

English, unlike Hindi, has tremendous rewards. Most high-paying or respectable jobs that do not break the law require English. Hindi films are written in English—the instructions in the screenplays are in English, and even the Hindi dialogue is transcribed in the Latin alphabet. Mumbai’s new film stars, like most educated Indians, find it easier to read Hindi if it is written this way. Higher education is mostly conducted in English.

So, millions of poor families spend their hard- earned money on educating their children in English, who then undergo considerable suffering to learn a language that is not a part of their environment; millions then realize that they cannot learn it well enough.

A natural resource appears to benefit all equally, but the upper classes are in the best position to corner a disproportionate share. The same happened with English. Even so, English helps some classes of the poor occupy professions that the elite have vacated.

For the Dalits, English was for long the Western counterforce that can neutralize Indian culture. It is to emphasize this point that the Dalit activist, Chandra Bhan Prasad, built a temple to the Goddess English in an impoverished village in the northern state of Uttar Pradesh. In his temple, there was an idol in robes, wearing a wide-brimmed hat. He planned to encourage young Dalit couples to include a ritual in their wedding ceremony in which they would sign the letters A, B, C, and D on a piece of paper. ‘That would be a promise they make that they will teach their children English,’ he told me. He also planned to fix a loudspeaker in the temple from which a recorded voice would chant the English alphabet, from A to Z, every day at 5 a.m.

His temple didn’t work out. It is hard for Dalits to pretend that the language belongs to them. In early 2011, I wrote in the New York Times, ‘English is the de facto national language of India. It is a bitter truth.’ It was true then, but I am not so certain any more.

Despite its rewards, English is a cultural failure in India even among the affluent. Its failure across India is far greater than the failure of Hindi in the South. Once, English was the language of the people who ran India. It was a time when class and money meant the same thing. India has changed. Even though English still has prestige, its influence is waning. It now belongs to a confused minority upper-middle class who have been retarded by English. India has to be the only society in the world whose educated upper-middle class does not speak any language with complete mastery. Many of us are stranded in English and we speak our mother tongues or the languages that raised us, very poorly. For instance, many of us cannot read high prose or even give a formal speech in an Indian language.

In this defeat of English, and all that it stood for, and all of us who had bet on it when we were children, lies another force that calms the poor. The mother tongue has risen in prominence, aided by technology. Now you need to only speak your language well to survive and even do business, you don’t need to even read very well.

The primary language of the new sahib is not English but his mother tongue. The world of the new economic and cultural elite is framed in Indian languages. In most of North India, the power of Hindi is total. In all southern states, the cultural influence of their independent languages is total. As a result, there is an emotional connection between the poor and the new icons, a bond that the average Indian used to share only with politicians, sports people, and actors. So, the rich are able to influence, persuade, and co-opt the poor in more efficient ways. The most influential news channels are today in Indian languages. Netflix India wants to be lowbrow in Indian languages. It and other platforms have created a new genre—heartland dramas. That is where ‘Bollywood’ too is headed in its fight for survival.

What is going on in new India is that a true pop culture is emerging that is unifying all the classes, except those who are westernized, who have in any case either migrated or vanished in other ways. The unification does not require a single Indian language; it only requires the absence of an anglicized worldview.

WHAT happens when we stop thinking in our mother tongue?

Meander a little with me before we come to ‘the point’. This is, after all, about language. I used to think in Malayalam and Tamil, though I always hoped only in English.

I was born in Kerala and raised in Chennai, a cuckoo among the crows. Like many people, I could speak only one dominant tongue at a time. As a boy I spoke fluent Tamil, and a type of Malayalam that Malayalis said was not Malayalam, and a heavily accented English. However, I read English the best, and as I began reading books, which were almost entirely works from the West, I changed. By the age of seventeen, I was moving away from Tamil and Malayalam, and I was beginning to think entirely in English.

There were hundreds of thousands like me across India, going through the same phase. It was obvious to us then that a bright future was a time and place that thought in English. Are there serious consequences when this happens, or is the specialness of language overrated? When I was around ten, during a monthly test in school, I was asked to write ‘the opposite gender of ram’. I was baffled when I realized that most of the class had got the alleged answer ‘ewe’. My answer was Sita. I still maintain that I was right. No one in Chennai had ever seen a ram, while Ram, or Rama as we used to call him, was everywhere. Everything else about English was like this—it was venerable at a distance but all wrong when it passed through me.

It was a medium of study and intellect but to use it in a casual conversation seemed comically arrogant. The act of speaking in English, in fact, was defamed as ‘Putting Peter’ in my circle. I was among the boys who carried out the defamation. My teachers were not fluent in English. When my parents spoke English, they, like all Malayalis, spoke English in Malayalam. The effect of not being fluent in English was that I was an insider in my home town. I belonged to my city and the place belonged to me. Not for a moment was I an amateur Indian. I could talk to thugs and policemen and slum dwellers and eunuchs. Also, come to think of it, even stray dogs didn’t faze me. I never even noticed them, even though as a kid I began to run at dawn.

Being Tamil, or being Malayali, is a distinct behavioural system and only those who thought in either of those languages could play a part in it. Maybe it had nothing to do with Tamil or Malayalam but with the absence of a world framed in English. For instance, when a Tamil Brahmin wanted to convey an insult, he would hedge the risk by putting it across as an ambiguous joke. Also, Tamils of my childhood had the propensity to use glee to show contempt. They would laugh hard, in an exaggerated way, at the jokes of the people they despised, especially their teachers or bosses. This is nothing remarkable or even unique. But it’s not an entirely insignificant thing either. All this I did not observe as a boy, when I used to think in Tamil. Observation is not the act of seeing; it is the act of remembering what has already been seen. Only when I stopped thinking in Tamil did I begin to actually see Chennai.

The Argentine writer Jorge Luis Borges once wrote that the one word we will not find in the Quran is ‘camel’. His implication was that ancient Arabs had no reason to mention what was very common in almost every frame of their existence, but an outsider sees what is too ordinary for the insider. (Borges was wrong; the Quran does mention ‘camel’. But I accept what he implies.)

As a child, I made no special effort to learn to read and write in Tamil or Malayalam. I used them only to think and speak. I could write only in English and Hindi, both foreign to me, Hindi more than English. Then, in 1987, a film called Nayakan by Mani Ratnam was released. I was thirteen and I thought it was the greatest film on Earth. I still believe it is one of the greatest. I decided that apart from being a journalist, I would also become a Tamil filmmaker. I began to write scripts for many Tamil films that I hoped would find takers, but all of them in English.

When I was sixteen, I thought the best way to enter the movie business was as a low-level actor. So when I heard that a film audition was underway for some extras, I went. The queue was long and the men looked impoverished and depressing, and the line had not moved after two hours, so I left, abandoning the idea of acting. I wrote more stories. I co-wrote one with the milkman. Nothing ever came out of them. The Tamil sphere of creativity offered me no prospects. So I let myself be colonized by the English language, by which I also mean a strange condition.

Compared to Indian languages, English has names for so many abstract things, labels, even lies, that they can be misunderstood as truths, like hypnosis, secularism, sexism, liberty, human rights, and plain nonsense like ‘quality time’ and ‘multitasking’. All these ideas might seem innocuous, even sensible. Some people might think these words capture innate human nature. They don’t. They are a way of thinking. And not long ago in India, people who thought like this were not merely artists and writers. They were the people who ran the country—they were prime ministers, ministers, judges, bureaucrats, editors, all of them shaping the nation at a time when a few influential people could run the country. Yes, most of them liked their samosas and some of them sang in their mother tongue, but the West was inside their heads. The West gave them their aesthetic, intellectual, and moral direction. They had this perception that the West was the pinnacle of the human race. This class and this way of thinking has been properly killed in India. I think the West is being killed even in the West. The death of this class has resulted in the unification of classes.

The richest Indian, Mukesh Ambani, in how he perceives wealth, marriage, and family values, his religious piety, his surrender to the mystical, is very similar to a poor or lower-middle-class Indian in the way a Tata was not. The poor can pretty much identify with all their major public figures. The depth of this belonging is best understood from the orphanhood of the other Indian, the amateur Indian.



The homeless amateur Indian

To ruin the chances of sleeping with them, rasp, ‘Is the science of climate change beyond dispute?’ They say things like ‘asparagus’ and ‘edamame’ and eat them, too. They used to love Aung San Suu Kyi, but not any more. They read very long articles in English that are called longform. They despise the word ‘infrastructure’ when uttered by a provincial man. But anything they hate they will consider forgiving if it is ‘sustainable’. They know what an ‘open’ relationship means. Many of them, especially in Mumbai, have ‘friends’ among street urchins. They hated giving their biometrics to their government but routinely do that for an American or European visa. They wish for diversity in plants and animals even though they themselves are a monoculture of identical ethical organisms spread across the informed world whose president was Barack Obama. At times, they hold candles and go somewhere. They were, for long, awkward in India, but they had their islands where they could escape the nation. Now there is nowhere to hide, not even in literature festivals. This is a government that is everywhere. So they feel uncertain in universities, think tanks, cultural bodies, journalism, theatre, art and mainstream cinema, activism, and in charitable works. They have lost beef too.

The worst truth of this new order is that they, who received the finest education and other opportunities, and who consider themselves the most intelligent and informed among Indians, have been shown as inaccurate, unreliable, and incompetent political analysts of their own nation. It appears that there is only one way left to use them as political forecasters. Listen carefully to what they have to say, for the outcome will be the very opposite. They are the amateur Indians.

They always were, since birth. The times when they strayed outside their safe houses, they did not know how to negotiate their own nation. What should they do when a government official asks for a bribe, what should they do when their car hits the bumper of another vehicle, how should they speak to a cop, what are the meanings of many words in their own mother tongues? But never before have state and society encroached into their islands so forcefully and decisively.

They are not alone anywhere any more. Nowhere in the malls, theatres, and restaurants can they be guaranteed a degree of refinement. In the theatres they must rise for the national anthem. And what should they do if a man is on the phone throughout the movie? They have heard stories of friends being punched just for objecting to the use of the phone in movie halls. Never before has the uncouth Indian been so empowered, and so affluent. There is no place he cannot afford, or be refused admittance to any more. In any case, some of the richest residential real estate in the country has for long been taken over by fanatic vegetarians. Now there are affluent residential colonies in all major cities where residents demand the construction of temples. What should the lofty people do? Where should the amateur Indian go?

They do consider themselves ‘global’, and everyone knows ‘global’ does not include Sudan or Mongolia. They will be able to obtain visas of the most advanced economies. But despite everything, all things considered, it is in India that life is the easiest, it is here that they are assured of good spots on the social mountain.

What must they do? How can they become as confident as the son of the soil, who has friends in the police commissioner’s office and in other government places, who knows when to stand firm during a traffic dispute and when to flee, and how to game the system. How to be Indian in India?



CHAPTER 9: HOW HATE UNITED RICH AND POOR
----------------------------------------

ONE afternoon in 2001, I went to meet ‘the first Hindu suicide squad’. As they stood in line for me, they tried not to laugh.

This is one reassuring aspect of India—even today the idea of a ‘Hindu suicide squad’ will bring a chuckle even to those who have been recruited. At least, at the time of going to press.

The squad was formed by a retired army officer, Colonel Jayant Chitale, in a distant Mumbai suburb. The colonel asked the boys to assemble in a deserted playground. When the colonel said they were the Hindu suicide squad and that they would terrorize ‘Lashkar-e-Toiba’, some of the boys giggled again.

Even though a Hindu suicide squad was unthinkable at the time, ‘Hindu nationalism’ had already become mainstream, chiefly brought into focus by one of the most successful revolutions in modern times—the demand to build a temple in the birthplace of Lord Ram, on the spot where stood the Babri Masjid, a grand mosque that opened in 1527 or 1528, depending on the scholar. A Hindu mob knowingly or unintentionally demolished it in December 1992. Violent riots followed. Then a significant event occurred.

On 27 February 2002, in a town called Godhra in Gujarat, a Muslim mob burned a train filled with Hindu political workers. The incident left 59 Hindus dead. Retaliatory riots ensued, which left over 800 Muslims and 200 Hindus dead. There is a perception that the state government of Gujarat had turned a blind eye in Muslim areas. Narendra Modi was the chief minister of the state then.

At the time of the Gujarat riots, there was a prevailing theory of human nature that Indians today may have completely forgotten. That a riot was a bad political idea. That people will punish a politician who was identified as the instigator. This view emerged from another fallacy—that voting was a moral act. Most of the English media of India believed that the more it linked Modi to the riots, the closer he would get to political oblivion. People who did not have this view had no central role in mainstream journalism of the time.

At the time of the riots, Modi was yet to contest an election. Since the riots, he has lost none.

Many years later, the television journalist Rajdeep Sardesai would admit to me, during a public interview, that people like him had demonized Modi for the riots and created the myth of a strongman. Modi flew with that gift, and he united the rich and the poor Hindus across most of India, first through a grouse against Muslims, and later by making patriotism a sacred duty.

WHEN I was a kid, I did not know a living Indian who was a patriot. It was as though patriotism was something that happened only to men who were long gone, like sepia. I can’t speak for the India in its early years after Independence as I am not as old as I look, but I can tell you that by the 1980s, you would not come by a living patriot for months. More than their Indianness, Indians felt stronger about their religion and language. And their caste, if they belonged to the upper caste. In fact, in all the jokes that involved men of various nationalities walking into a bar, the Indian was the biggest fool, unless there was a Pakistani. We did not think very highly of ourselves. We were expected to ‘love’ India, somehow, and also flee India. It is not that we felt nothing. No doubt we wanted India to win a cricket match (though in Madras, as Chennai was known then, you could not be sure of this when India played the West Indies). I spent hours going through the pages of Encyclopaedia Britannica searching for a compliment about India, or something more appealing than our antiquity.

One day, sometime in the 80s, during the convocation ceremony at the Indian Institute of Technology, the chief guest said, ‘Brain drain is better than brain in the drain.’ The joke spread across Madras at the speed of sound, making us shake with laughter, pin code after pin code. (I am sorry this joke will appear one more time in the book.) IN 2001, just a few months after my meeting with the Hindu suicide squad (all of whom I suspect are still alive), the filmmaker Karan Johar would tell me in the course of an interview why he had included the national anthem in Kabhi Kushi Kabhie Gham, his film that had released that year. ‘It was just part of the narration. I don’t think the Indian youth is so patriotic that I should attract them through nationalism.’ He knew India’s modern youth better than most people because he actually made money out of them. Also, at the time, you didn’t have to be Karan Johar to believe that the Indian youth was not patriotic; it was an obvious fact. And Johar said of them, ‘they are totally focused on themselves. You show money, they like it.’ In 2003, I went around Mumbai and asked young people, as in people my age, simple questions about the nation. I met a young Muslim woman who worked as a hairstylist. ‘You are a Muslim?’ ‘Yes,’ she said. ‘Who is Shah Bano?’ I asked referring to the Indian Muslim woman who, in 1985, won a landmark Supreme Court case for alimony after divorce, a ruling that upheld her right under secular law but was overturned by the Rajiv Gandhi government to appease theological Muslims in a stupid political move that would help the rise of Hindu nationalism. The hairstylist shrugged in the mirror. She had never heard of Shah Bano. ‘Who is Narendra Modi?’ I asked her, about the man who was then the chief minister of Gujarat, where just over a year ago there had been riots between Hindus and Muslims that would influence Indian politics for many years to come. ‘Is he someone important?’ she asked. ‘He owns Modi Xerox,’ I said because I had always wanted to say that to someone. ‘Oh,’ she said in a manner that suggested she had just learned something important.

In other such places, the posh young were as ignorant: On Kashmir: ‘Jinnah wanted to give Kashmir to India but Pakistan stole it and we are trying to get it back.’ On Ayodhya: ‘Someone destroyed a temple.’ On Hindutva: ‘Never heard of it.’

But very soon things began to change. The middle class got interested in their nation. And they even got honest about their love. Some of those guys who had laughed at the joke of the convocation chief guest do not laugh at such jokes any more. They have become patriots, who are probably people who do not laugh at themselves any more. Today, most Indians across all classes are patriots.

How did this happen? How did a generation grow proud of their nation? It was not as though India became impressive in any special way. In fact, since the 80s, even though India has progressed, many developing nations have left us far behind in quality of life. India continues to be a difficult place to live in. The best thing money and success can buy continues to be a golden visa out of here. So what made us patriotic?

THE first signs of the revival of patriotism appeared in

an improbable person: there is a type of talented Indian who lives in the United States with his austere wife to whom he lost his virginity, and has two children who are good at spelling. He walks with a mild slouch. He is still intimidated by white waiters, but not black waiters. In an elevator, chiefly in an elevator, he suspects he is probably small. He is acquainted with the word ‘generalize’ as something other people should not do. He is at times a she. He is fundamentally a good person by almost all the definitions of that human condition— he is against genocide, burning people alive, including Muslims, and stabbing children, including Muslim children. He loves the idea of a smart, tough, and proud Hindu. He was not always so traditional and patriotic. I know that because he was one of the boys I grew up with.

He will give many reasons why he is a patriot now, he will give abstract reasons. He will say love is abstract, love is inevitable. It is not, in reality. Love is calculated, always. In America’s caste system, he is nowhere at the top. In fact, at times he feels he is at the bottom. There are moments, he knows, when brown is the new black. Back home he was something by virtue of his birth, lineage, and education, which was clear to all in plain sight. And the riff-raff, which knew its place, readily granted him this, unlike in the United States. That is why he loves India. That is why the elite of all Third World nations who live in the West are deeply in love with their homelands. The first wave of modern Indian patriotism emanated from the US in the late 90s. People of my generation felt this through our former classmates who had moved to America for education and had just started work there. Our first reaction was surprise because these new patriots had shown no signs in school or college that they so loved their nation. But the NRI, we found out, had grown fiercely in love not only with his nation but also religion, culture, and language. He was radicalized in America by a society that did not deem him a member of its upper crust. All this came to the surface when India tested five nuclear devices in Pokhran, in May 1998. It stunned the world. The Americans had had no clue this was coming. Their satellites had been hoodwinked. India had exhibited not only its nuclear science but also its cunning (which is rare). Indians were ecstatic. The good news travelled at the speed of bad news. For the first time in my life, I heard Indians who had settled in America stating how ‘proud’ they were of their nation. Many of them will deny today how ‘proud’ they were because today they are evolved, ecologically conscious people with similar friends. But for many others, since that day, the Indian nuclear programme never lost its manliness.

The way people of ‘Indian origin’ in America reacted confused me at first. Guys who had never read a newspaper when they were in India spoke of the ‘strategic advantages’ of a nuclear India, and girls who used to read J. Krishnamurti and send postcards to villagers affected by the Narmada dam, rejoiced in the manly power of a nuclear India.

Those of my generation who had stayed back in India did not feel so strongly—at least in 1998. But that, too, changed rapidly with the rise of the urban villager. By this I mean a person who has the trappings of a big-city flâneur, who uses that daft expression ‘cosmopolitan’ for social diversity in urban spaces, but is in reality someone who finds comfort in social hierarchy, big family, tradition, sects, home, and also in contempt for people who are different from him. In some ways, the second wave of patriotism was a middle-class Hindu response to a collective middle- class fear of Islam and its followers.

This force was widespread, but did not consume everyone. In 2003, when the bankrupt Maharashtra government compensated for its cluelessness by making it mandatory to play the National Anthem in cinema halls, one could sit in defiance as the sacred song played and not be assaulted by the public. I used to sit. I told myself that if someone asked me to stand, I would say, ‘Tell me the language in which the anthem is written’ (a lot of people don’t know the answer) or I would tap into a forgotten conspiracy theory that Tagore wrote the song in praise of King George V, who was visiting. (The first line of the anthem is indeed weird even for a poem. ‘Victory to the Lord of the minds of the people, the Dispenser of India’s destiny,’ does sound like a tribute to a monarch.) But, increasingly, it was getting difficult to sit, until it became plain unsafe to remain seated or get into humorous banter about the anthem.

There were still swathes of urban Indians who were not consumed by patriotism. They were converted by suave sanctimonious intellectuals, who said all the right things but were such preachy annoying people that they drove a generation to be their exact opposite. A recurring theme in this book is that people tend to take the opposite positions of the people they despise.

THE poor of India have almost no reasons to love their nation. They would have been better off if the British were still ruling us. They used to say this but they can’t any more. Today the nation is sacred and there are things they cannot say.

Once, the rich could only use patriotism to recruit cheap soldiers, but today they use it to tame and control the poor. India is a goddess; you speak no ill of a goddess. Poverty is no one’s fault. In September 2024, a spiritual figure in Tamil Nadu told the poor that poverty was their fault, their karma, they probably did some horrible things in their past lives. The poor disrupted the event. But what that man said, it is a lingering doubt in the poor. That, and the powers of persuasion of the rich, have converted the vast poor into some sort of patriots. After Hinduism, it is another cultural unification of the poor and the rich.

A defining nature of this unification is that we as a nation only talk about useless issues; we are most passionate about useless issues. We have an immense stamina for useless issues.

ONE January, I was in New Zealand, a place of such relentless pastoral beauty that its stray patches of ugliness always turned out to be golf courses. There was so much street joy as people kissed, ran, biked, and hiked. From there I followed the most important news in India. And for an instant how it felt was that India, too, like New Zealand, had solved all the problems of basic survival and therefore was obsessed with frivolous issues.

The fact, though, is that our air is poison, we are stranded for half our lives in traffic congestion, we lurk in spatial ugliness and chaos. Our children grow up with very low racial self-esteem. Yet, the greatest public outrages of India are about abstract or obscure matters, issues that will not improve the quality of life; that will, in fact, only make living here worse.

One Friday night, in a very affluent Gurugram club called Quorum, I asked why this was so. I asked the question to the politician Subramanian Swamy, who once said Indians who are not Hindu should not be allowed to vote unless they declare that their ancestors were Hindus. I asked him why do Indians have immense political stamina for useless issues?

He understood the spirit of what I meant by ‘useless’—issues that will not improve the lives and prospects of an overwhelming majority of Indians. Religious and other cultural identities are not ‘useless’ as such but in a poor country they are primarily the domains of the upper classes. How, then, have they transmitted this disease of identity so easily down the classes?

Swamy told me, correctly, that identity is not a trivial thing, that religious, caste, or national identity matter to a vast section of Indians, including the poor. But then that was not the substance of my question. ‘Why’ was my question. Why is it that a majority of the nation that lives in conditions worse than some animals in New Zealand, wastes its political energies on issues that matter to Swamy? Why don’t people riot for air-conditioned transport and water and electricity and free schools more often than they riot for useless abstractions? Or at least peacefully vote for these issues, as the people of Delhi had in the years that the Aam Aadmi Party was in power from 2013 to 2020?

Some powerful forces are at play apart from the most obvious element, which is that the upper classes have a disproportionate control over storytelling. The fundamental nature of stories and storytellers, too, are responsible.

A story is a very corrupt thing. A story is not an event; a story is an interesting event. And herein lie all our problems. What is interesting in a story is not essentially what is important in life. At times the two do meet, but that is rare.

This is why politics is the way it is, and why democracy itself is doomed in the long run. The most captivating stories are not necessarily true, nor what will make our lives better. People ‘identify’ with stories not out of wisdom or clarity but impelled by that highly influential force—misunderstanding. Clarity is often inconvenient, while misunderstanding is a pleasurable massage of prejudice. For when we misunderstand something, we attribute to what we get wrong all that we want it to be.

Far more important is the nature of storytellers. The effectiveness of a good storyteller does not emerge from virtues like sanity, sobriety, clarity, stability, or that complex but useful thing, compassion. The insane, the melancholic, the bitter, the enraged, and the innately melodramatic tell the best stories. They are too good for more sober or moral storytellers who try to win people over through common sense, say through the promise of better infrastructure, schools, and hospitals.

Even those politicians who know the game, who try hard to be interesting and dramatic to achieve their moral end, cannot sustain their power if they are only pretending to be insane. They just cannot be as effective as people of a very different mental state from which nothing good can ever come.

But can’t issues coexist? Can’t religious identity coexist with duller things like hygiene and infrastructure? The problem, as we see in India, is that political focus is a zero-sum game; the political stamina of the public is finite. Everything is at the expense of something else. Dull but transformative issues, like matters that affect the quality of life, often cannot compete with more emotional yet essentially useless issues like religion or caste.

There is a parallel in India’s space programme. Its fans have raised a question right from the start when its expense—hence its moral justification in a poor country—was challenged. They said, why can’t the space programme coexist with the eradication of malaria? Should a nation wait to kill all its mosquitoes before it sends metal into space?

But then, considering the fact that rocket science is easier than eradicating poverty or malaria, we arrive at the very reason why societies do some sexy things—to hide the fact that they cannot achieve difficult things. All of the exotic issues of Indian politics exist not because Indians so love their culture. They exist because it is easier to demolish a mosque or build a temple than it is to enforce standardized intelligent road design across the nation.

The poor are not innocent. They are drawn to esoteric and sensational issues more than anything that will transform their lives. So, it is easy for the rich to recruit them as the face of a useless national emotion. I have seen the poor riot over the death of a film star or not being allowed to enter a movie hall. Almost never about the condition of a public school.

But then isn’t it true that matters of great relevance can hide within useless issues? For instance, riots are not only about religion and caste, but also about reducing competition for jobs by displacing one sect, and even usurping their lands. So, impractical issues themselves are fronts for more practical matters. But then why do we hide more important things in less important things?

Also, the fact that Indians have finally managed to love India, but they don’t really love other Indians. I hope that one day a cunning humanitarian billionaire will create breakthrough visual effects that will create the illusion of God, who will then order Indians to have some concern for others; drive in their lanes, and stop shitting on the road. And also, tip more.

ONCE, I argued in the Hindustan Times that nationalism can do a lot of good, like clean the air. My chief argument was that the history of other nations should teach us that nationalism always begins as dangerous pride but slowly becomes something more genuine in the form of true love for a nation, and when that happens, pride is replaced by shame, a national shame for all its flaws. And people then solve their difficult problems. But I have withdrawn that opinion. I am unable to hold that view any more, watching India struggle with the issue of quality of life and mask all its failures in bullshit. Meanwhile, the new urban Indian patriot has the swag of a guy who belongs to a global gang of ascending hardliners. But this camaraderie is delusional. A curious way of the world is that there is no such thing as the global right wing. And that is a blessing for the poor.

IN the British comedy Fleabag, a young man wakes up to find his girlfriend masturbating while watching a video of Barack Obama. The moment is not just about a woman’s response to Obama. We can infer that she believes in climate change and the liberation of Palestine.

Far less mesmerizing men than Obama have been global heroes—like Hugo Chávez, who was once treated like a rock star at Jawaharlal Nehru University. But all such public figures of various nationalities have one thing in common—they are humanitarians. Strongmen, on the other hand, are usually local heroes, and if the world outside their fiefs cares about them at all, it is usually as villains. There appears to be much in common between ‘right-wing’ heroes of various nations, but they are never in alliance, like humanitarians can be.

Is there a global WhatsApp group of strongmen who exchange jokes about Obama, Macron, Marx, women, and The Guardian? Is there an intellectual festival for patriots from different nations where a Brahmin, a white, and an Arab will argue what a nuisance poor migrants are, and how cultural diversity is just a device to get cheap waiters?

That does not exist. Not because people don’t think along these lines. They do. It is the ‘global’ aspect of such a congregation that seems ridiculous. There is something odd about the idea of a ‘global nationalist’. By nature, the ‘left wing’ is global and the ‘right wing’ is local. Of late, new Indian patriots have been trying to change this; they have been trying to form global alliances with conservatives in advanced economies. After all, the Indian patriot today is more suave than his earlier avatar. New nationalists are culturally and linguistically comprehensible to the West, and they are successful people in respectable professions. But the global right is a futile exercise. There will never be such a thing.

So?

The impossibility of a global ‘right’ ensures that ‘the world’ is always on the side of the poor of a region, and other victims of the strong. That puts pressure on the ruling class of a region to do what appears to be humane.

After the Narendra Modi government integrated Kashmir with India beyond any ambiguity, the modern Indian patriot imagined US President Donald Trump would cheer. As though it was natural that Trump and Modi should get along because the same newspapers are critical of their leadership. Patriots even imagined that a global network of strongmen would support the nationalistic mojo of the move. But supporting Modi on Kashmir was clearly not the priority of ‘conservatives’ across the world. Even so, New York Times, Washington Post, The Guardian, BBC, and scores of Western liberal intellectuals who knew very little about India’s northernmost state magically acquired enough wisdom to bemoan the ‘suspension of freedom’ and ‘the violation of human rights’ and ‘the suffering’ of Kashmiris.

Meanwhile, Fox News and The Sun did not magically acquire any right-wing wisdom—that the poor of Kashmir have been ‘liberated’ from their crooked elite, trauma merchants, terrorist middlemen, and economic stagnation.

The ‘left’ is a low-stakes human hive of hearts that sees the world as a human hive of victims. The ‘right’ is a high-stakes preoccupation with what is locally relevant. The ‘left’ is a monoculture of a European idea; the ‘right’ is culture. I know Indian Hindus who despise Muslims in their own nation, but their hearts truly ache for faraway Palestinians. All elites are like parents—conservative at home, where the stakes are high, and liberal elsewhere, an abstract place that is not as important as home.

(It is not only the social right that is helplessly local. Increasingly, the economic right, too, is shedding interests any more. During his first presidency, Trump told the United Nations, ‘We reject the ideology of globalism and we embrace the doctrine of patriotism.’ In his second term, he has almost finished off free trade, at least at the time of going to press.)

There are, of course, a few global right-wing figures. Hitler, for instance, has for long been a mascot for groups that wish to terminate cultural diversity in society. Russia’s Vladimir Putin has fans in the West who post videos of his motorcade, of his walks down corridors in slow motion, and of his bare- chested horse rides. But these fans are a small fringe compared to the numbers, spread, and diversity of the fans of humanitarian figures. As I have explained, there are powerful reasons why there is no such thing as the global right. Any wound that is global will automatically be a left movement.



CHAPTER 10: WHY THE POOR LOVE POLITICIANS (Or How Politicians Protect Us From the Poor)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FOR a few days in 2003, I stayed in a sprawling gracious home on the outskirts of Harare, as a paying guest of a middle-aged white couple. I was there to cover the cricket World Cup, which Zimbabwe was co-hosting. All around us, the nation was falling apart. The currency had already collapsed. Zimbabweans called their dollar Ferrari, ‘because it is red and it vanishes’. I think for the first time in my life I saw a phrase play out in real life—I saw people stand in long lines for hours for bread, actual bread. As an Indian, I thought I had seen all forms of poverty. But this was new. People stole anything made of metal, like road signs. The official exchange rate of the currency was also an official joke, to the extent that when foreigners landed at the Harare airport and tried to exchange their meaningful currencies, the officials who manned the currency counters had to work hard to hold back their laughter, their nostrils quivering. Because the official exchange rate was about fifty Zimbabwean dollars to a US dollar. After exchanging the currencies, when the foreigners stepped out on the streets, they would realize that an American dollar was worth more than 2,000 Zimbabwean dollars. One morning, my hostess, who was an athletic and energetic woman, told me that she was distraught by ‘the situation’. She was not referring to the economy though. The situation was that Zimbabwe had become unsafe for white landlords like her. Zimbabwean president Robert Mugabe wanted them out. The World Cup did not dissuade him from saying what he had always said—that white farmers must vacate their farms and leave Zimbabwe.

Over breakfast in the kitchen, my hostess began to tell me how much the world hated Robert Mugabe. ‘Even the blacks (of Zimbabwe) hate him,’ she said.

That, I was not willing to believe.

I was twenty-eight and knew very little about African politics (I barely knew Indian politics). But the Third World brain somehow understands other Third World brains. My instinct told me that between the posh and their despot, the poor will always hate the posh more. Also, my general rule for understanding other nations is that a major public figure who is loathed by New York Times and The Guardian usually has mass support among his own people, who look like him and talk like him. I am not saying that is fair; I am just saying that is the nature of the world. The world was such even then, just that the intellectuals were yet to notice it.

I told my hostess that I did not believe that the blacks of Zimbabwe hated Mugabe. She found my statement ridiculous. It was a time when people believed that a hypothesis that was not morally correct could never be the truth.

Two young black housemaids hovered around, serving us. It had never occurred to my hostess to just ask her maids if they liked Mugabe. It was, to her, like asking newly empowered Germans in Nazi Germany whether they liked Hitler. Now, faced with an unthinkable hypothesis from a brown foreigner, she decided to ask the black maids what they thought of Mugabe.

The hostess told the maids softly, with unnerving seriousness, that she had a question for which she needed a truthful answer.‘Don’t think I will be offended by your answer, don’t think I’m your employer, don’t think of anything, just speak the truth. Do you like Robert Mugabe?’

The maids looked at each other and giggled. They did not answer at first because they were smart. My host kept nudging them. Finally, they replied. They said that they liked him. ‘He is for us,’ one of the maids said.

My host was stunned. And disgusted. She would later tell me that the maids were ingrates, that she had fed them, clothed them, paid them handsome salaries, and treated them with respect, yet they loved that thug Mugabe.

The blacks, even then, did not adore Mugabe. They did blame him for their economic mess but they also admired the way he was driving out the whites, the feudal lords.

Across the world, that is the nature of the relationship between the poor and their politicians.

Politicians are usually wealthy themselves but their identity is not that of rich men. They are provincial strongmen, rustic headmen, chieftains, the central foes of the classy. Also, their corruption and their ill-gotten wealth is evidence that they are useful men, especially to a person who is dependent on the state. They are, culturally, closer to the poor than intellectuals, humanitarians, and other kinds of activists can ever be. They are more relatable to the poor than even fabulous poor writers can ever be.

I do not refute an Indian sentiment that all our problems come from the poor quality of politicians. If only there was a magical way in which the Indian politician was not like the average Indian at all, we would have been such a wonderful place. But the fact is that the corrupt politician is useful to the poor (and in that way saves us from their ire).

The people who have proved to be the most useful to India’s poor have always been those who wished to overtly profit by transforming their lives — non-banking finance companies that replaced street moneylenders; mobile phone manufacturers who made cheap computers; telecom tycoons who have made calls almost free; social media billionaires. And, politicians who will do anything to win elections, including actually doing good.

The American filmmaker and poet, Jonas Mekas, once said, ‘In the very end, civilizations perish because they listen to their politicians and not to their poets.’ Like some beautiful things writers say, this is a complete lie. It is my belief that if only people knew the true character of male poets and all the politicians, they will probably stop reading poetry but they will still vote.

WHEN the Indian politician Sharad Yadav died in 2023, I wondered what exactly he did all his life. Every day, when he set out to work, what was his work? What did he want? One of the worst things about dying in India is the poor quality of obituaries. Most tributes termed Yadav a ‘socialist leader’. What does it mean? Among the meaningless words in Indian political reporting, ‘socialist’ is on a par with ‘secularist’.

Yadav was a successful man, of course. Yet, obituaries struggled to explain what he did for other people. His two remarkable feats are that he became a member of parliament at the age of twenty-seven and contributed to the political pressure that forced V. P. Singh as prime minister to adopt the recommendations of the Mandal Commission, which opened reservations for a broad band of Other Backward Classes, thereby defeating the whole point of quotas.

It is likely that what Yadav did most of his life was what most politicians do most of their lives—he indulged in politics, which is a mixture of deal-making, lobbying, cobbling together alliances, compromising and plotting to win elections. He was caught up in the process of politics. This differs from the formal objective of politics, which is to improve the lives of a huge number of people.

In the 20,000 odd days Yadav was a politician, most of them were spent in politicking, which is merely a process. I would have said that this occupied all his political life, but I am not counting the few days when he campaigned, won polls, gave speeches that influenced people or policy, as was required to transform society.

Politicking has a sense of direction and gravitas because of its objective, which is to transform society, but politicians spend very little time on the transformation part. In fact, it can be said of most politicians that if it were not for the harm they cause, their professional lives would have been entirely without any impact. Imagine a profession where its professionals raise funds to make a movie, forge alliances to raise funds, plot ways to go about it, analyse what would make the film project work, but never actually make the film.

But then, incredibly, progress is not all there is to politics. Life itself is not only about better roads, clean air, and prosperity. A society is also about grouses. Classical politicking politicians reassure large communities that they will not be superseded by other communities. And there is value in this. That is why Indians have always rewarded cultural guardians above road builders. You can build a world-class metro system and change how millions commute, and still lose an election. But, as we saw in the previous chapter, if you play to the grouses of the people, you would be more successful.

The politicking politician is the creation of the politicking Indian. But the politicking politician, though devoting only a small fraction of his time to the welfare of people, gets them stuff in ways others cannot. Very simply, he bribes them. And Indians have been extracting bigger and bigger bribes.

IN 2006, a group of graduates from the vaunted Indian Institutes of Technology (IIT) decided to enter Tamil Nadu politics. They posted on their website an introduction to their ideology, in English—‘Reality is a continuum. Knowledge system, in shortest, is fragmentation imposed upon the continuum of reality.’ They were trying, through the language of a humanities professor, to defy the chief minister of Tamil Nadu, the great veteran, Karunanidhi. Karunanidhi offered every poor family of Tamil Nadu a free colour television. Of course, he won the elections.

As he handed over the TVs, he surely remembered a time when it was not so expensive to impress the poor. In the early 80s, the chief minister of Tamil Nadu, M. G. Ramachandran, made it legal for two people to ride on a bicycle, a traffic offence until then. The masses of the South Indian state rejoiced in the streets and, not for the first time, they hailed their chief minister as the true hero of the poor.

It was that cheap to bribe the poor then. Minor charities were passed off as reforms. The poor, who did not really believe that their condition could be improved by human intervention, accepted these gestures as great acts of kindness. When MGR died in 1987, hundreds of thousands of people attended his funeral, even though he had not made them as rich as he had made himself.

But those days are over—of offering an Indian a piece of his own bicycle as alms, and free rice and cheap milk, and plastic pots (when they asked for water). In 2016, Karunanidhi promised the poor free or subsidized smartphones, tablets, and laptops; high-speed internet connections; also 10 GB of free download every month, waiver of education loans, and homes.

His rival, J. Jayalalithaa, who once had him arrested in the middle of the night, from his bed, without letting him even ‘wear my underwear’ as he screamed, told the masses, ‘Amma knows what her children need.’ And she promised, among other things, 8 gm of gold ‘for every bride’, presumably every poor bride—that’s most of them anyway. By Jayalalithaa’s standards, 8 gm of gold is just a false tooth. But for most Tamil brides, 8 gm of gold means a free mangalsutra—that glorified symbol of Indian marriage. Jayalalithaa also offered free laptops with internet connections for school students, free electricity, and subsidized mopeds for women.

The great actor Kamal Haasan told Tamils, if you accept bribes, ‘You will only get a thief as your leader.’ But the logic of Tamils has always been that they would get a thief anyway, so why not share a fraction of the booty.

For decades, Jayalalithaa and Karunanidhi took turns to rule poorly. Once, their most effective policy decision was that when Karunanidhi would assume power, he would change all bus route designations from ‘J’ to ‘K’, and when Jayalalithaa would come to power, she would change ‘K’ back to ‘J’. Both were widely perceived as corrupt. Jayalalithaa was the more entertaining of the two, though. She arranged men in geometric formations and made them bow to her. Women loved her.

Things are no different in any other state in the country. All major political parties need to lure voters in crooked or at least sly ways to win elections. The elite are disgusted not only by the unofficial cash bribes but also by profligate programmes that are giving away increasingly expensive alms. But this is nothing but hollow moral outrage. The wages are so low in the nation, opportunities so meagre, and the private sector so exploitative, that crooked and bad politics have often turned out to be humanitarian acts. The Indian voter backs a corrupt politician because the rogue gets things done for him, at least in the short term.

Politicians also routinely save the poor from the ideals of the westernized amateur Indian. In fact, politicians saved a crucial innovation for the poor from being abolished by privacy paranoids as we shall see in the next chapter.



CHAPTER 11: HOW A BILLIONAIRE AND CAREER POLITICIANS SAVED AADHAAR FROM AMATEUR INDIANS
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A few years ago, the philanthropist, and probably the wealthiest Indian to ever moderate a panel discussion, Rohini Nilekani was steering a discussion on ‘Writing Under Threat’ at a literature festival, when she was ambushed by one of the speakers, Sujatha Gidla, the Dalit writer who describes herself as ‘an untouchable’ to shame India for its caste system. (Once, Gidla told a man in a bar in Atlanta that she was ‘an untouchable’, and the perplexed American flirted, ‘but you are so touchable.’) Gidla told Rohini Nilekani, ‘I looked you up on Google, it says you are the wife of (she was unfamiliar with the name that follows) Nandan Neel-kay-nee, the chief of Infosys. Then I wondered how you got to be the moderator of the session about writing under threat. What experience do you have with threats? I would have thought you are on the other side—those who threaten. Isn’t it true that Infosys recently laid off tens of thousands of employees?’ (Gidla herself was a techie who was laid off in the United States.)

Gidla then attacked India’s unique identity system, Aadhaar, because its architect was the moderator’s husband, Nandan Nilekani, the co-founder of Infosys. Aadhaar was created to identify every Indian, especially those who did not have documents. On its basis, hundreds of millions of Indians could create bank accounts into which the government directly transferred cash benefits. Aadhaar remains one of the great humanitarian ventures of the modern world. (It is remarkable that Aadhaar and Al-Qaeda mean the same thing, which is ‘foundation’, and it is even more remarkable that its neurotic foes have missed this tweetable fact.)

Activists hated Aadhaar for reasons Gidla stated to Rohini Nilekani. ‘It is mass surveillance, it will affect political activism or anything we do against government.’ She then quoted another writer who had said Indians now have to get the permission of the government to write books, which was untrue, but she made it sound as though it was not only a common fact but also the fault of Nandan Nilekani. While the assault was underway, Rohini Nilekani laughed unhappily, and once shifted her own chair by a few inches. But when she got a chance to respond, she said, ‘I just want to ask you one thing. I am surprised you are talking about me as a wife and not an individual....’ There was applause from the audience, which made Gidla pick up the mike as though she wished to show she had a deadly response, but she did not say anything. Later, she would complain on Twitter (as X was then known) that the audience had many Brahminical women ‘with fancy sunglasses who came to see Rohini Nilekani’. In the world, according to Gidla, the Nilekanis and their class of friends in fancy sunglasses were the omens of class oppression. But, could it be that she had missed the more lethal omens of our age that hide in plain sight?

For a girl who was raised in poverty and who had seen at first hand how what was meant to be the alms meant for her never really reached her, and how a whole industry of middlemen kept her hungry, she was unable to see or accept the power of Aadhaar, which has and will ensure that government subsidies reach those they are meant for. She was happy to use an American social security number but she perceived the Indian unique ID number as a ‘threat to freedom of speech’. Even though she has fashioned herself as a Dalit humiliator of Brahmins, her alarm at Aadhaar is, in actuality, a repetition of the modern shlokas the elite have framed to push their discomfort with a system that extracts information out of them in the national interest without offering them any direct or immediate service. Like other Dalit activists, she had become a minion in an upper-class campaign against Aadhaar.

All of India’s political, economic, and spiritual history is proof that when the poor and the rich have the same ideas, it means the rich have co-opted the poor. And Gidla too was, without realizing it, bowing to a creed set by those who, in another reality, would have been her urbane feudal lords. She might as well have rocked in her chair and chanted the Manusmriti. Why is it that someone like Sujatha Gidla, who has had the experience of abject poverty, not on the side of the Nilekanis who hail from the Indian urban middle class, worked salaried jobs in their youth, and made their wealth from the cleanest industry in India—software— and are among the very very few Indian billionaires who have devoted a great portion of their wealth and time to improve the lives of Indians.

How did Gidla, in despising the tech elite, become a foot soldier of the very affluent socialist idealists who kept her ancestors in free India poor?

THE protests against Aadhaar did not begin as a battle for privacy. They began in the intense dislike social activists had for billionaires who strayed into humanitarian activities—their forte. So the campaign against Aadhaar was, very simply, a turf war between humanitarian middlemen and Nilekani, whom the activists saw as an outsider who had ‘spent no time in the villages’. The turf war was passed off as a noble cause through the addition of some reasonable concerns and useful delusions. (The most influential activists are not frauds, they are people who see visions.) Soon, they attracted people who are attracted to anything negative, including the young with a sense of failure, who have been cast aside by the modern material world and so derive their self-worth from activism.

In 2018, I went to meet Nandan Nilekani in his home in Bengaluru, which was a squat building on an upper-middle-class street. It was a week before the assembly elections in Karnataka were due.

At the exact moment I arrived outside the large home, a swarm of BJP supporters passed by on the street screaming campaign stuff, as though to taunt Nilekani, who contested the Lok Sabha seat in Bengaluru in 2014 on an Indian National Congress ticket—a time when all that a perennially successful Saraswat Brahmin who wished to taste defeat as a life experience had to do was join the Congress party.

I had hoped for a tech-billionaire home where I would stare at a retina scanner to make the large metal gates slide open. Instead, his was an austere household where you first left your footwear outside. (His office, which was a few lanes away, was even more low-tech—all it had by way of security was a guard who sat outside the gate with a register in which you entered your name and a fake mobile number.) The living room framed a sun-lit courtyard, and the home was filled with the sound of an unseen grandchild who was trying to talk.

Nandan Nilekani appeared with damp hair after a fresh bath bearing his ‘reaching-out’ face, which was a mixture of mild suspicion, patience, and some inner mirth. Every time I met him, I felt there was a joke running inside his head that he did not wish to share. He had been investing in start-ups since 2014, ‘just to get a sense of how the start-up world works.’

He also started a fund called Fundamentum which would invest in companies ‘at the point of take-off’, like Travel Triangle, a technology platform designed to be the travel agent for travel agents. ‘On the public side,’ he was the innovation and policy adviser to the National Payments Corporation of India, an organization promoted by the Reserve Bank of India to help every Indian access digital payment systems. He was one of the principal funders of a new university in Bengaluru—the Indian Institute of Human Settlements, which hoped to ‘create professionals with a holistic view of urban issues’. He was involved in a non-profit that was trying to figure out the smartest ways to run cities, and he was also chairman of a foundation that would figure out ‘how to use tech to advance learning for children’.

This seemed like more work than what people with home loans endured. Why couldn’t he just go to Switzerland and enjoy his money?

‘What I do is not a lot,’ he said. ‘Sounds like a lot. I don’t have an executive role in any of these things. I do have lots of free time; I go on vacations, I spend time with my family; I am not a type-A person working eighteen hours a day.’ But he did not have too many hobbies. ‘I don’t read fiction,’ he said. It then occurred to him to apologize to the interviewer, who was a novelist. ‘I stopped reading fiction at the age of thirty. Anyway, all the good novels were written by then.’ He apologized again for the insult. He watched a lot of television and particularly loved Silicon Valley, a comedy about a bunch of programmers who ran a start-up. His favourite character was the satanic Canadian programmer, Gilfoyle. ‘Silicon Valley is spot on,’ he said, bearing a very different view from Elon Musk, who had said that programmers were much smarter than what the series portrayed. On a par with Silicon Valley, Nilekani said, was Billions. ‘What Silicon Valley is to Silicon Valley, Billions is to East Coast capitalists.’ THE alpha men of Billions have a low opinion of consensus because consensus is often the triumph of the second-best idea. Billionaires generally have a low opinion of consensus, as a requirement to get anything done. But when Nilekani left the corporate world in 2009 and entered government as the chairman of the Unique Identification Authority of India, a post he held for five years, he became a big believer in consensus. ‘Consensus is not the same as unanimity. Not everyone agrees. That is part of the game. A broad agreement needs to be achieved. I reached out to every player in the ecosystem. I met everyone under the sun: politicians, bureaucrats, activists, journalists. ’

One of them was probably me, which resulted in my most silent lunch ever. I had requested him for an interview, which he declined, but as a tactical consolation invited me to lunch at his government- sanctioned home in Lutyens’ Delhi. And two South Indians who were not good at small talk with strangers ate vegetarian thalis through long periods of silence, and the moment I scooped up the last bite, he said, ‘OK, good meeting you.’

No matter what he did, there were always people who hated the idea of the government, which they called the ‘state’ in an ominous way, collecting biometrics. ‘When I met NGOs, I was surprised by their hostility. I thought they were there to solve problems, and Aadhaar was only going to help them, but they were extremely hostile.’ A strand of hostility surely emanated from the habitual resentment of activists for billionaire philanthropy, because the idea that retired capitalists could solve problems which austere career socialists could not for decades was deeply disconcerting to them.

WHEN Hindu patriots, or anyone who eats samosas three times a week, talk of culture, it is as though no one else has culture. When people who eat avocados talk of conscience, it is as though no one else has conscience.

In the beginning, when it appeared that Aadhaar was meant for the poor, the elite, barring a handful of activists, did not have serious concerns. But when it became clear under the Modi government that the state wished to make Aadhaar mandatory for all citizens, they developed a public conscience. Outrage spread through the ranks of the elite and the war against Aadhaar became a war for freedom from the state’s peering eye.

Was it a mistake to make Aadhaar mandatory for the middle class and the rich?

Nilekani told me that some important effects of Aadhaar could not be achieved if it was not mandatory for all. ‘Take the PAN card problem. There is a lot of duplication. We had a big racket going on. We had people giving one PAN for IPO, one for taxes, one for jewellery. With Aadhaar you are eliminating all the fake PANs and money laundering. The unique thing about Aadhaar is that it is, well, unique. This can work only if 100 per cent of the PAN cards are linked. If you make it voluntary and only 80 per cent link, the 20 per cent will be the fake PAN guys. So there was a logic to making it mandatory for PAN.’ But he was not so sure about making Aadhaar mandatory for opening bank accounts or getting mobile phone service.

‘I’m not hung up on that.’

When Aadhaar became mandatory, for all practical purposes, for the middle class that paid taxes, Nilekani transformed from being the middle-class hero to a despised man. But he was dismissive of this transformation. ‘Some attacks have been personal, but the people who do this are a microscopic minority. We should not be swayed by the Twitter universe, half of them are bots or something. When I interact with people, and I interact with a lot of people, there is widespread appreciation. Actually, now I am not hurt at all. Earlier it was disturbing. I was not used to this sort of unvarnished criticism. But now I have been at it for nine years. It doesn’t bother me.’

Rohini Nilekani considered it her greatest fortune that she was not on social media. ‘Because of that I am not exposed to the vitriol.’ She said, ‘I made a smart decision by not being on the social media. I was a strong critic of Facebook right from the start. I am a bit of an idealist and I thought you are commoditising friendship.’ But she was aware of the abuse, though, and was disgusted by it. ‘Even Dalai Lama gets vitriol.’ ‘I know some of the opponents to Aadhaar reasonably well,’ she said, ‘and have had occasion to partner with some of them and even support them in the past in a different context. They are people with a genuine concern for society. But I have seen in the Indian NGO sector that they want ideologically perfect solutions from the start. But change is an iterative process, with ups and downs, and many surprises too. So I wish there could be more openness. And here I am only talking about the people who care about a good society, only they are of interest to me here, not the professional trolls or those who make a noise out of personal agendas or enmities. The lesson of the past century is that states can become totalitarian and markets can become too powerful. And when the state and market join together, the citizen and the samaaj can be oppressed. So these concerns are real. But this is not the last century and there are new opportunities that need to be looked at without fear.’

Optimism was a central feature of her husband, she said. She meant it in a good way, of course, but optimism has the quality of psychosis. Optimism took Nilekani far in life, but it also made him believe in his chances in electoral politics. ‘One thing about politics is that you have to psyche yourself into believing that you are going to win,’ he said. ‘There is no other way. It is such an arduous thing, it is physically and emotionally draining. Self-doubt doesn’t work in this game.’

Rohini Nilekani said, ‘Sometimes optimists may be deliberately denying something. Sometimes people who are not optimists are caught in analysis-paralysis. So we see that people who are not optimists are often not doers either.’

Even though Nandan Nilekani dismissed the social media villains of Aadhaar as inconsequential, he did feel at one point that the ceaseless campaign against the project on social media was influencing the mainstream, and that the mission itself might be destroyed. This was soon after Modi’s spectacular victory in 2014. Aadhaar had been a campaign issue, and Modi had criticized the project, which the Congress had birthed. After Modi’s victory, Nilekani ‘was alarmed’ when he read in the news media that the Modi government might scrap Aadhaar. ‘I went and met him in his residence, in 7 Race Course Road. We spent thirty minutes talking, and he asked some very good questions—chiefly about law and migrants. I told him it was not a programme of citizenship but of identity. I think he was already convinced. I hesitate to take the credit of convincing him.’

This was not the first time Nilekani had met Modi to pitch Aadhaar. In 2012, when Modi was the chief minister of Gujarat, the two had met. Modi had rolled out Aadhaar in his state. As prime minister, despite his public posturing against Aadhaar, he was receptive. ‘I told him it is important that you have a law. Aadhaar needed the legitimacy of being sanctioned by law. I told him Aadhaar will reduce the deficit and you can use the money for development. Ever since he has been a staunch supporter of Aadhaar. He is tech-savvy. He is always thinking about how tech can be used.’

One quality that Nilekani liked in politicians was a quality that was not a part of the public perception of them—‘They are actually very open-minded to ideas. They are always under the gun to achieve results, so they are open-minded. If you have a solution, they listen.’ Eventually, the speed and scale at which the Modi government took Aadhaar forward surprised him. 1.2 billion Indians have Aadhaar now, and its popularity has spread through the power of word of mouth. Over 2 billion authentications are done every month using Aadhaar.

‘Last year there were 3 billion transactions on Aadhaar. 100 thousand crores have been transferred on Aadhaar, and the government’s savings, according to an estimate, are ₹83,000 crore. Total investment on Aadhaar was ₹9,000 crore. On that lifetime investment, we have already got five times that amount in savings.’ From my conversations with the kind of people who depend on the government for their survival, it appeared that Aadhaar was a significant addition to their lives. ‘Denial of service because of the failure of Aadhaar system is of course unacceptable. But this system is only going to get better and better,’ Nilekani said.

RIGHT from the start, or at least from that point in its origins when Nilekani was involved, Aadhaar was not going to be just a unique number representing the attributes of a person. ‘One thing was clear to me. We will build this as a platform to allow innovation to happen. We were inspired by the internet and GPS, which were essentially publicly funded infrastructure. Today we don’t think of them as government platforms but platforms where private innovation occurs.’ That was what he believed would happen to Aadhaar, a gigantic platform where private companies would find ways to transact or engage with hundreds of millions of Indians. This would be done through software programs authorized to talk to and use Aadhaar’s data to perform various functions like digital money transfers and verification of government-issued documents.

The collection of such programs designed to use Aadhaar was known as India Stack, which was owned by a non-government organization called iSpirit. The sophisticated middle-class resentment for Aadhaar, he said, was a consequence of its imitation of the West. ‘The Indian elite has always been influenced by imported ideas. Earlier there was Fabian socialism; communism, Marx. Today imported ideas are ones that are warranted for Western situations. Ideas without due thought for the Indian context. It’s colonization of the mind. Just because a guy from an Ivy League in the West is saying something, it doesn’t mean he makes sense in an Indian situation. A lot of liberal education is focused on a dystopian view of the future. So there is a dystopian view of GMO, and of other tech, all bad stuff, like Blade Runner or something. That’s the problem. In today’s India there is a volcano of aspirations. A billion people want to do stuff, they want a better life, you can’t keep giving the same dystopian stuff, can’t keep saying you can’t do this, can’t do that. They are going to say, look give us something, man. Even if it doesn’t work give it to us.’

That humans will hate intelligent machines is a recurring artistic prophecy. But art’s portrayal of how exactly we will hate machines has never been convincing. Humans will probably hate machines in abstract ways, maybe through liberal outrage, through hating digital platforms like Aadhaar. Nilekani makes a sound. It is probably a scoff to suggest I am overthinking, and that I am overestimating the opposition to Aadhaar. ‘You are making it sound like a big thing, it is not such a big thing.’

He said that the whole game of activists was to influence the minds of the Supreme Court, but it was a battle they had already lost. ‘The judges have understood Aadhaar very well,’ he said. The judges eventually ruled that all ideological opposition to Aadhaar is subordinate to every Indian’s right to identity and prosperity.

Nilekani wished to affect the destiny of Indians, but not as a leader any more. He wanted to be the backdrop. He will not be a politician again, he will not be ‘in the front’ again. ‘My political career is over. It’s what is called in the internet world “fast fail”. You try something and you realize quickly this is not going to work. I didn’t want to be the Rajya Sabha sort of politician. It had to be through the legitimacy of winning an election. But I soon realized I don’t have any competitive advantage. Also, politics is murky. I have zero interest now. The episode was traumatic. When you lose an election it is like a public rebuff, a public rejection. But it was good, it has left me a better person. I have no confusion now about my role in life.’



CHAPTER 12: LET THE POOR HAVE FUN

How technology enslaved the poor

Hours after the Indian government introduced its own tablet computer, as though in tribute, Steve Jobs died. There were no other omens. In fact, on 5 October 2011 and the weeks that followed, the Ministry of Human Resource Development appeared to have reason to be confident and proud of the tablet, which was called Aakash. There was somewhat congratulatory world attention for days. At just over ₹2,000 Aakash would be the cheapest tablet in the world and would be distributed to millions of Indian students at prices that were even cheaper. But then they would have got what they paid for.

Aakash was a disaster. Its production was hit by delays and there were a range of quality issues. Eventually, Chinese manufacturers claimed that the company that had been contracted by India to produce Aakash had simply bought Chinese tablets off the shelves and changed the name.

The real issue was that the Indian government was not in the business of making computers.

When Aakash was facing production delays, Narendra Modi, who was yet to become prime minister, taunted the ruling Congress party by asking why Aakash (which means ‘sky’) ‘has not come down to Earth’. In response, the man who was then the human resources development minister, Kapil Sibal, said that because Modi lives in ‘the netherworld’ he has to be shown the sky. So Sibal sent an Aakash tablet to Modi, but Modi returned it without opening the wrapping. Aakash had political significance not only because it was cheap, but also because it was a computer. The computer, which then meant a desktop or a laptop, had a mythical quality to it in the India of the time. To millions, it promised transformation, a generational leap. It was a symbol of prestige much the way a pen in a shirt pocket was not long ago. Chandrababu Naidu, when he was chief minister of Andhra Pradesh, took care to appear in public carrying a laptop. (After some electoral defeats, though, Naidu wondered if the laptop was making him look too modern, and he abandoned it.)

The combination of extreme poverty and worship of digital technology in India had, over two decades or so, inspired an industry of local and foreign not-for- profit organizations to make India a centre of cheap innovation that could transform the developing world. Aakash was a product of this attempt. But then Aakash turned out to be yet another reminder from India that to achieve good deeds in a developing nation, noble intentions are usually not as efficient as the natural forces of materialism, corporate self-interest, and the market.

IN the late 90s and early aughts, as a writer for Wired.com and Outlook magazine, I met many innovators and humanitarians who attempted to make cheap computers, translate or transliterate languages, and even invent cheap wireless internet. But in the end, all these were achieved not by rural activists or ‘socialists’ or even philanthropists. These were achieved by Blackberry, Apple, Samsung, and the scores of copycat manufacturers, and by Google, Intel, and other giant corporations that were driven to achieve profits.

Activism finds it hard to forgive the tech industry for this. Any corporation doing any good, accidentally or intentionally, is an encroachment into the turf of career humanitarians. Many times, the biggest menace of the poor is the beautiful fragile heart of the amateur Indian. We could witness the whole drama from very close when Facebook came up with the ‘internet for the poor’, and activist mullahs passed a fatwa against it, citing an obscure theology: ‘net neutrality’.

It is yet another second-rate alliteration in our life that poorly defines a powerful idea. The term ‘net neutrality’ is borrowed from an academic paper published in 2003 by the lawyer and scholar Tim Wu entitled ‘Network Neutrality, Broadband Discrimination’. The broad present-day definition of ‘net neutrality’ is that a service provider should be barred by law from giving faster or cheaper user access to some software applications that are willing to pay more for the special treatment. Many support this commandment. Many, like the co-founder of Paypal, Peter Thiel, and the co-founder of Netscape and an early investor in Facebook, Marc Andreessen, felt that net neutrality was an almost obsolete idea (even around 2015), and they did not like the idea of any more government regulation. They were right about its obsolescence. Once, Barack Obama used to talk about it. Now no one cares. There are violations of net neutrality everywhere.

Tim Wu did argue why net neutrality was important, but he did not venerate the idea as much as present day internet-Marxists who used his invention as their slogan. His paper, in fact, pointed to the follies of absolutist positions, and argued that the world, and probably every age, has to figure out a meaningful definition of net neutrality.

In 2014, the Law Commission of India and Foundation for Media Professionals organized a ‘closed-door meeting’ of journalists and activists with the chairman of the commission to discuss whether the government should regulate the media. I was in the room. Towards the end, an activist, who is often mistaken for a journalist, asked the moderator, a senior journalist, to open discussions on net neutrality. At the time, it was an abstract subject and only Indians who followed the American media, or at least the conscientious comedian John Oliver, had some knowledge about the matter. The senior journalist said he had no idea what net neutrality was. The activist said that it was a principle that demanded that the service provider should not offer any preferential treatment to any websites or software applications. Ten seconds after the senior journalist learned what net neutrality is, he framed a bleak concern—in the future, large corporations may diminish others by paying a fee to service providers that others cannot afford, and that governments may try to turn off the speeds of problematic media websites. Ten seconds was all it took for the journalist to frame his moral stand, because nothing in this world is really ever new.

Tens of thousands of people across the world instinctively choose the side he chose. That is the type. You tell them Facebook wants to give free internet to the poor but it will decide what website they will have access to, and they will say it is like Saudi Arabia funding madrasas for poor Muslims. You tell them scientists are trying to genetically engineer vegetables in the lab, they will say we should not tamper with nature even though modern farming is far from ‘natural’. You tell them we need to have beautiful airports, they will say air-conditioned railway stations first.

In 2015, and for a few years that followed, the idea of net neutrality infected Indians who are susceptible to activism. They feared that large corporations would pay service providers to make their offerings many times faster than the others. Start-ups worried that they would be crushed, and their paranoia played into the hands of radicals who made a bizarre improvisation based on an obtuse and theological interpretation of net neutrality and they issued a fatwa against Facebook’s free internet for the poor (then called Free Basics) as a violator of the sacred definition of net neutrality because it was barricading the internet. Free Basics provided a scaled-down version of the internet to anyone with a mobile phone subscription. A feature phone would be enough to be connected to the internet. In India, it was available only to Reliance subscribers but Facebook (it was not yet called Meta then) said that it would partner with any service provider and that it would include in its parallel internet any application. There was no commercial transaction between Facebook and the telecom companies, nor did it charge a fee to the applications that wished to be on Free Basics.

At the time, in 2015 and 2016, a billion Indians were not connected to the internet because it was too expensive for them, but most of them had at least feature phones, so, in theory, all of India could be online through Free Basics. But the mullahs of net neutrality said that Free Basics was unethical because it violated a sacred code—that the internet should not be fenced in, and a limited internet for the poor was fencing in (but many of the same activists were using the iPhone which is a fenced-in set-up). Also, the activists suspected that Facebook was trying to hijack the internet. They frequently quoted one Indonesian study that warned that the poor were so dumb they thought Facebook was the internet.

At the heart of the fight was the idea that the internet was everything or nothing, that a limited version of it did not align with the spirit of the internet. A limited internet offered by one corporation, especially Facebook, was a corruption of the very idea of the internet. But wasn’t all this technicality subordinate to the fact that the poor have a right to some internet? The activists said, let the service providers or the government offer free MBs every month to the poor. But then those companies asked, why should they give anything free? They didn’t have the endless resources of Facebook. Too bad, the activists implied, no free internet then to the poor.

A lazy, neurotic suspicion of the large corporation was behind the obtuse alarm over Free Basics. But the very strength of the parallel internet for the poor was that it was corporate strategy. Mark Zuckerberg tried his best to give it a humanitarian spin, which may not be wholly a lie, but I did hope the venture was not purely altruistic. Facebook in search of new market segments would end up doing more good than internet altruists. It already had. His idea of an internet for the poor triggered serious corporate and social movements to find other ways to connect the poor.

One dawn in December 2015, I spoke to Mark Zuckerberg on the phone. ‘The intensity,’ of the opposition to Free Basics, ‘is certainly unique to India,’ Zuckerberg told me, ‘why it is surprising, apart from the fact that it happened at all, is that it happened in the country that has the biggest need to connect its people.’

Zuckerberg did not deny that he had business interests in connecting the world. ‘In the long term, as more people get online, that would undoubtedly increase our business opportunities.’ But, he said, ‘everybody’ and not just Facebook would gain from that.

When Facebook first created the free internet for the poor, it was called internet.org, which was a system that let the corporation pick a handful of websites it would feature. The activists thought it gave too much power to Facebook. The activists said there were other ways of giving free internet access to the poor. Ways that they did not bother to create. For instance, they said a service provider could give away some free data to the poor every month. But then how would a service provider know who was poor? Historically, the Indian elite have benefited more from subsidies for the poor than the poor themselves. Also, are the activists comfortable with the thought that the free data can be used up in a day on pornography?

In the end, the Indian government banned Free Basics. A few months later, Mukesh Ambani’s Jio was launched. It made data very cheap. The gargantuan profits from oil and other businesses of Reliance Industries subsidized Jio. Millions of poor joined the internet for the first time.

After the tech mullahs sabotaged Free Basics, I took to asking them publicly, on social media, every few months as to what they were doing to take the internet to the poor. They never had any answers. Eventually, they started pointing to Jio to say how the markets had ‘organically’ lowered the price of data so much that a section of the poor could now afford the internet. After all that good boy activism against Facebook, the activists had to depend on Mukesh Ambani to do social service.

The flaw in Free Basics was not that it was a creation of a corporation, but that the corporation thought like an activist. In picking a lesser internet for the poor, Facebook picked grim sites that imparted education and health advice. Like many technological innovations for the poor, Free Basics presumed that what was important to the poor were the very grave aspects of life, like information and health. The fact is that the poor have best embraced a technology when it is fun. But then fun is expensive as it usually contains heavy data. Free Basics had plumbing lessons, but no songs or films or porn or games. It did not have the very things that made the internet popular.

These very companies that transform the lives of the poor when they do not intend to, turn out to be less effective in enriching the poor when they intend to be good boys. Often, tech billionaires and executives feel that they must treat tech as something deeply noble and serious in order to substantiate its importance in an impoverished nation. This has led them astray. The early efforts of Google and Facebook, for instance, to connect a billion Indians who did not have access to the internet, were couched in social service.

They couldn’t do without the farmer story. Generally, when people who wish to transform India through the internet talk about their plans, they tend to tell a story that goes something like this: A poor farmer is about to sell his crop very cheap when someone, often a smart adolescent granddaughter, checks the market price online, and the farmer makes a more informed decision.

When I was twenty and found a job with a lifestyle magazine, one of my first assignments was that I had to call some of the richest people in Chennai and ask them what they usually had for breakfast. The first wealthy man I called told me, ‘I don’t eat gold biscuits.’ It was a well-deserved reprimand for presuming that rich people were somehow different from other humans. It is equally ridiculous to presume that what poor people want from the internet is lessons in plumbing. IN 2008, I went to see a magic machine called the Replicator. It was a machine that could make other machines. It was an early version of the 3D printer that had a clever set of scanners and cutters. In theory, it could replicate a mobile phone. In theory, it could replicate itself.

This machine was housed in a lab built by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology’s Centre for Bits and Atoms. There were only six such labs in the world and I had gone to see the one in India. The lab was in a desolate impoverished home in an impoverished village outside Pune, the kind of village where boys who went to school had the flies of their shorts held together by their mamas’ safety-pins. (Mine, too, was on some days of my childhood.) The six labs were an innovation of MIT professor Neil Gershenfeld, to usher in a fabrication revolution that could enable regular people to create almost anything they want. The other five labs were in affluent regions of affluent nations, but the one in India had to be in a poor village. ‘This is the typical Indian village,’ an Indian who worked on the Replicator told me. ‘If you can do something meaningful in Pabal, you can do something meaningful anywhere in this country. It is poor, drought stricken, and is not on a highway.’ His team used the Replicator to make a ‘milk tester’ to tell if milk had gone sour and a one-watt bulb. This was the parallel cinema version of tech.

The Indian government has traditionally imagine that it is obscene to talk about science and technology without mentioning how useful it will be for the poor. For instance, India took care to pitch its space programme, which was in reality a pursuit of naive prestige, as a tool to foretell weather to the farmers. Once, Prime Minister Narendra Modi even managed to make the spacefaring Elon Musk seem dull after a visit to Tesla’s campus when he tweeted ‘Enjoyed discussion on how battery technology can help farmers’.

It is not always true that entertainment is the collateral consequence of progress; progress, often, is the collateral benefit of the pursuit of pleasure. Quality of life is as much an assortment of happy frivolities as it is the bare essentials of survival. And India is a perpetual reminder that a lot of good—even the sombre sociological stuff—can come from people setting out in pursuit of joy.

What the poor want from a technological revolution is probably best understood by watching the way they react to electricity. They do not crave electricity so they can keep newborns warm in incubators. They want it for the simple pleasure of having light and other conveniences in their lives on demand.

The extraordinary triumph of the mobile phone among India’s poor stemmed from its ability to enable a most mundane human need, which is to chat with other people. And when the poor chat, it is not always about curing a child of diarrhoea.



CHAPTER 13: WHAT MONEYLENDERS KNOW
----------------------------------

EVERY time my mother pawned a gold bangle, she became extravagant. She would march to the grocery store to clear her debts. The half-naked shopkeeper, who usually sat on a sack of rice eating his own jaggery, grew wary when she approached because she was always buying goods on loan. But when he saw that she had cash rolled in her fist, he quickly became servile, which was the only way people in Madras then knew how to show respect. In her hour of glory, my mother turned feudal and enquired after the shopkeeper’s life, imitating Indira Gandhi speaking to the survivors of a natural calamity. And she went home carrying cake, biscuits, chips, squash, Horlicks, laddoos, and rusk. Exactly those items. Even in the 80s, there weren’t many things to buy, at least in India. My sister and I would eat the loot as my mother slowly turned silent and morose. After all, she had lost more of her gold. Then nothing moved in the house except three mouths: my sister and I munched food as my mother mumbled about her gold, about how she would get it all back. A woman muttering a reassurance to herself, that was the first thing in my life that could make my heart ache. Some days she would say that when I grew up, I would retrieve the gold for her. It was my first foreboding of adulthood—that I had to one day retrieve family jewels. I said I will, but she knew perhaps, even if I didn’t, that her gold would soon be melted and transformed into an entirely different object to adorn the neck of a luckier woman.

Every time, her objective for mortgaging gold was more honourable than the joy of buying junk food, which was never called ‘junk food’ those days because it was unthinkable to call any food ‘junk’. Even junk was never called junk. Her objective was always ‘school fees’, and there was nothing more honourable in my Madras of the 80s than school fees.

Kunjamma had eleven gold bangles and a chain when she moved to Madras from Kerala. In the first years of raising her children, she lost all her gold to pawnbrokers. She took to wearing my sister’s thin gold waistband, also known as aranyanam, around her neck, until that went too. Every time, she borrowed at 120 per cent annual interest, pawning her gold as collateral, and every time she was confident that she would repay the pawnbroker and retrieve her gold, and every time she failed to pay in time until the cost of retrieval was higher than the price of her gold.

The pawnbrokers never cheated my mother. (They didn’t have to.) I remember them as Jain men with clean white faces the shape of a five-paise coin and silky hair combed in the good-boy style; men who had the astonishing ability to sit forever on thin white cushions with their legs tucked under their arses. All Jain pawnbrokers in Madras were called ‘Marwadis’ even though not all of them were from Marwar. They had gorgeous daughters.

Eventually, in 1991, India itself would behave like my mother. It would go broke with no dollars left to buy ‘essential commodities’, and it would pawn its gold with a blade, a term for pawnbrokers I will explain a little later. When India surrendered its gold many Indians were humiliated, but in our home we found it funny.

About three decades later, I told my mother that my first journalistic book would include a long profile of a family of pawnbrokers. And that I would argue that they are more humane than those who are widely considered humanitarians. Maybe she was amused. She did lose all her gold to pawnbrokers. A part of my childhood was indeed financed by them at an extraordinary interest rate. But then our lives were so entwined with them, we didn’t quite know if we loved them or hated them.

I discovered that current Indian laws ensured the pawnbroker cannot simply liquidate gold upon default. Today, if my mother had defaulted as she used to, the pawnbroker would have to sell the gold at market price, deduct the loan and interest, and give the rest of the money to my mother. How beautiful. (How does this happen? How do good things happen in India? We will come to that in the penultimate chapter.)

I told my mother that pawnbrokers are not called that any more. ‘Blades,’ she said, ‘they are called blades.’

Malayalis called the Jain pawnbrokers by the English word, ‘blade’. Because they bled you to death with their interest rates.

‘They have another name now. This whole business is called “gold-loan”.’

She considered the euphemism without emotion. I told her not all pawnbrokers were Jains. In fact, the biggest were Malayali Christians. Like us, almost.

THE Protestants of Kerala are from a few ancient sects, and have for several generations been a part of the state’s social and economic upper crust. They even believe, overtly, that they are from the upper castes. Because, they say, their ancestors were Brahmins. They are mostly concentrated in the central districts. They are the old money. Like this family that has assembled in a small village in Kerala.

Down a little lane here, in a large gracious home of an ancient Orthodox Syrian Christian household, a feast is underway. The air is filled with laughter and banter. The crowd in the sunlit dining room are family; not friends. If they had been friends, they would have been of a uniform social and economic type, as adult friends are these days. The surprising thing about a large family gathering is that it assembles all sorts of social classes, even though everyone may have the same nose.

On the rough wooden table there is food that only a home in Kerala can achieve. There are the red embers of fried fish, crispy beef, archipelagos of chicken in thick coconut gravy, and fresh vegetables from the garden. All through this, a silent matriarch sits watching her brood. She appears to recognize only those she loves. I will only refer to them as ‘the Family’ and not by their famous family name because I do not have the permission to identify them. (I had met them as a part of a book project that did not work.) They have assembled to remember the matriarch’s husband, who surveys the gathering from several walls with an amiable face. Let us call him the Patriarch. He was a shrewd and affable moneylender. Strangely for a Malayali, he had a morbid fear of elephants. He was a farmer who owned hundreds of acres of fertile land in Kerala and Tamil Nadu, and invested in several businesses. He even invested in wind energy. ‘Every time the wind blows,’ he told a friend, ‘I will make money.’ But his core, his masterpiece, was lending loans against gold.

Like other big moneylenders of his time, the Patriarch was socially respected and theologically condemned. In Kerala’s Christian community, especially, the profession was stigmatized. In my mother’s farming household, near Kollam, when farm workers needed money they used to surrender their metal plates as guarantee for money, but the household did not charge any interest. (Because charging interest was a Christian sin, many affluent Christian homes never lent any money when the poor came asking during an emergency.) As it was the case in many religions, usury, or lending money for interest, was condemned in Christianity, which had taken too seriously a single pronouncement of Jesus Christ: ‘Lend and expect nothing in return.’

Across the millennia that Christianity spanned in Europe, moneylending was a disgraceful act and even punished with execution. Around 1515, a German called Jakob Fugger, who is regarded the richest man who ever lived by some estimations, changed this. He bribed Pope Leo, who legitimized profiting from moneylending. To be precise, the pope legitimized lending that involved ‘labour, cost or risk’. There is no human activity that does not involve at least one of these three qualities. In this way, Fugger and the corrupt pope may have set the foundations of Western capitalism.

The Christian rehabilitation of moneylending did not last very long (a future pope would reverse Leo’s order). In any case, despite the backing of a corrupt pope, the stigma never really left the profession of moneylending. For centuries, people in Kerala attributed any disease among moneylenders to divine retribution for moneylending. Even today, people make that connection. Not just the poor customers of the Family but even their social equals, their acquaintances whisper to them that all their sorrow emerges from their profession. The passports of some of the Patriarch’s grandchildren do not carry the family name. Because people behind the counters in any airport in Kerala or Tamil Nadu always react to that name. There is scorn at times, there is servility, too, but there is always a movement across the stranger’s face, muscle groups shift under the weight of opinion. The name is concealed in school records too.

The stigma, especially in Kerala, is odd. Kerala is an ancient, mature, and advanced market for gold loans. In fact, the very reason why Kerala’s gold ornaments are so chunky, ugly, and austere is that historically their purpose was for mortgage more than adornment. Malayalis buy gold to leverage it, to pawn it, and not so much to flaunt it, at least flaunt it the way Tamil women do. (The late chief minister of Tamil Nadu Jayalalithaa even had a gold belt.)

In Kerala, it is easier to find hoardings that announce jewellers and gold loans than it is to find a woman bedecked in gold. Of all the Indian states there is least shame in pawning gold in Kerala. Yet, all the shame is on the pawnbroker. This is changing though. The modern times have somewhat rehabilitated the Family. But one dark quality of the stigma has not left. It is the supernatural, mystical dimension to moneylending. The curse of the poor. ‘Your family has a lot of curses from people. It’s people’s tears,’ a friend told a member of the Family, a young woman who is expected to play a crucial role in the future of the business. The Family are deep believers in god, so they never dismiss any mystical phenomenon as completely baseless. They themselves have wondered at various points in their lives whether the Family’s line of business was indeed wrong.

Large banks do not face any stigma. In my life, I have paid over ten million rupees to the ICICI bank as interest payments alone, but they do not worry about my curse even though I do curse them a lot. Even pure micro-finance companies don’t have any stigma; in fact, they are viewed as pious and one of their pioneers even won the Nobel Peace Prize. Only a particular kind of moneylender faces the curse even though what they have done is diminish the power and influence of the traditional street-level moneylender who used to lend at over 100 per cent a year, like the pawnbrokers my mother used to borrow from and the gigantic Pathan who was my father’s financier.

The Patriarch, like most rich men of his time, was deeply religious, and was accorded great respect by the religious establishments to which he donated enormous sums. There were days when the Patriarch rose from sleep and decided that he had to give a sermon. He would then lure his sons into taking him to a church, where he would make the request to the priest. It is unusual for a man who is not a priest to be allowed to give a sermon, but it was hard for most churches in Kerala to refuse the Patriarch.

He believed that he had something to offer precisely because he was not a man of god, because he was a man of the material world, a merchant. And lay people connected with him and with what he said the way they didn’t with the priest. (This is also what intellectuals, who are a kind of high priests, do not get or accept about the nature of the relationship between ordinary people and billionaires.)

The Patriarch knew that people wanted to know his secret, how he had made so much money. He knew he was a good brand ambassador of God. He made God look very useful. On the other hand, solemn priests made God look mean and dangerous. A regular theme in the Patriarch’s sermons was that God forgave ‘instantly’. He sold the idea of ‘instant’ more than the forgiving bit. All that people had to do was plead to God to forgive, and God forgave ‘instantly’. Standing in a church, he was advocating a direct communion with God, which he thought was efficient as opposed to going through a spiritual bureaucracy.

The Patriarch, in his time, may have wondered why no saint ever cared to explain to people that if there is anyone who wishes you well, wishes you quick and sustained prosperity, and a very long economic life, it is your moneylender. Because he expects the return of his principal. But then it is in the nature of people to imagine friends as well-wishers and moneylenders as people who wish ruin. It is very odd that people must think this way because the fact, often, is the inverse. The Family, today, has thousands of branches across India, most of them in Kerala where there is one every 300 metres. This is not a monopoly. They are not the biggest gold loan company in Kerala. In that state, there is one gold loan branch every 100 metres. In most of Tamil Nadu, there is one every kilometre.

Every branch follows a procedure that is a response to an emotion the Family believes is at the core of the gold-loan business. That when a person comes to pawn her gold, it is a very serious moment in her life. The staff is instructed to be solemn. They are to abandon laughter, banter, and the phone, and respect the excruciating solemnity of the moment even if it were just a trivial bangle that the person has brought. It will deeply move my mother —to know that pawnbrokers always knew how she felt. It is possible that even the Jains of Madras were not being contemptuous of her when they sat with their serious faces inspecting my mother’s jewels—maybe they were trying to look solemn, hence respectful of my mother’s bad moment.

The Family also followed another important layer of decorum, which is a response to their belief that the moneylender, no matter in which village, municipality, or era he might be set in, is essentially a neighbour. So the staff is instructed to make small talk with the customers. It is not designed to extract information, but the flow of information is an inevitable consequence of well-meaning small talk. The Family doesn’t accept gold without trying to understand why a customer is pawning her gold. When the reasons are not honourable, or not clear, they even refuse.

It is not unusual in Kerala for a drunkard to walk in with his wife’s gold. Such people are sent back (with their gold). The larger idea here is caution, and the idea behind caution is to erase all association of exploitation from the action of pawning gold, even if the exploiter is not always the pawnbroker himself, even if the exploiter is a lover or a husband, or a thief. Women should not pawn gold unless they wish to. This is how the Family rehabilitated the very idea of pawning gold.

There is much that the staff in a gold loan branch finds out about a customer even though their mandate is to close the transaction in five minutes. The transaction involves asking amiable questions, accepting the gold with a serious face, putting it in a pouch, weighing it and checking it to ensure it is indeed gold, and giving away about 90 per cent of its value in cash, or if the amount exceeds ₹2,000, writing a check or wiring the amount.

According to an old employee, when the Patriarch visited any of his branches he never sat in the manager’s chair. Most people who mention this to me misunderstood it as an act of great humility but the fact is that the capitalists of the Patriarch’s time were efficient humans; their actions usually had deeper purpose than false notions of humility. Why wouldn’t a Malayali moneylender sit on a manager’s chair when he visited a branch office? Another old employee of the Family told me the reason.

If a customer walked in and the Patriarch did not know the customer’s name, it would be a disgrace. The moneylenders of another time attached considerable importance to familiarity. Even today, the Family tries to retain that rustic feel, even if a branch is in the heart of Mumbai.

The Patriarch did not mean to lead a life that would protect him from the revolutions of the poor, but inadvertently he illustrated why the poor do not ‘kill’ the rich.

Moral ideas like the importance of family, sacrifice, culture, religion, nation, and the idea that a community is more important than an individual, are ideas that primarily favour the rich but they are enticing to the poor who wish to emulate or imitate the rich.

The rich who think these ideas are tricks to control the poor usually fail. The rich who believe in these ideas and whose own lives are hard evidence that they believe in these ideas are the truly powerful transmitters of social morals that restrain the poor from overturning the order.

The self-interest of a merchant is beneficial to society. But the feudal act of direct intervention through charity is not only useful to the poor, its optics also protect the rich.

Austerity is not a trick. It is a form of intelligence.


    The state of wealthiness is not a caste; being posh is a caste. The poor despise symbols of money far less than symbols of class.



Why wealthy south Indians are austere

All great comedians, when they appear in public, look very serious. There can be several ordinary reasons for this. For instance, the default human face is grave. (In fact, the face of joy itself is not a smile but an impassive face.) Also, only bores and people with personality disorders try to be funny all the time; comedians, also professional cartoonists, are funny only when they need to be. There is a more important reason, though. Their seriousness is also a deliberate projection. Comedians like to appear serious because they don’t want to be trivialized as clowns. This mechanism is also the reason why companies that are constantly criticized for being unethical, even when they are not particularly so, are inclined to project themselves through hyper-morality. Mining and oil and gas companies show excessive and not entirely dishonest concern for nature the way Apple Inc. does not. And people who do not have a talent for natural goodness, try to make exaggerated guesses about what being good might be—they are the wokes. Most good people are just those who are afraid of being seen as bad. Which brings us to why some Indian billionaires fly economy.

FOR the first time in my life I ask, ‘Are you a billionaire?’ The wealthy Malayali chuckles. It is probably the first time in his life that he is asked the question. He has many businesses but most of his revenues come from ‘gold loans’—from interests on small loans made to the poor. In his vaults are over 53 tonnes of gold, or about a sixth of the gold reserves of the United Kingdom, and he pumps out over ₹100 crore every working day into the economy. His personal wealth is not widely known beyond the fact that he is, like many wealthy Malayalis and contrary to wealthy Punjabis, far richer than he portrays.

Sitting in his dimly lit office in Thiruvananthapuram, he tries to wriggle out of the question through a practised answer:

‘I am just a custodian of people’s wealth.’

Seasoned journalists may maintain a respectful silence to ensure that he does not get off the hook, but it would be a futile exercise. He will wait you out. Or ask a general knowledge question about some debt crisis, and peer into your eyes as though to ascertain whether you are about to bullshit.

He does not look like a billionaire, but that is because no one in the world really looks like a billionaire. I will call him Malayali Billionaire because if he sells all his businesses and assets today he would easily be worth more than a billion American dollars. In Kerala, most men look as though they are wearing someone else’s trousers and shirts. But this man is a rare Malayali whose clothes appear to belong to him.

One morning, at an airport, I found myself in a queue right behind him. When we entered the plane, we saw in the sparse business section the familiar sight of male faces gaping with a mixture of pride and embarrassment as a file of passengers walked towards the lesser seats. The gold loan billionaire, too, surprisingly, walked past the business class. When he was about to sink into the cramped economy seat, he was thrilled to be recognized by some people. He was thrilled not because of being identified, but because he was found sitting in the economy class.

‘When people leave their gold in our vaults and when they pay interest on their own gold, they are also paying for our business expenses. When I know this, I cannot travel business,’ he tells me. Also, when others know this fact, it is not wise for him to travel business. BY the standards of furniture, the business class seat is an ugly and low-tech chair, and a mediocre bed, yet it has come to symbolize so much about capitalism, extravagance, and inequality. As a result, its renunciation by a person, especially one who can afford the seat, becomes an overrated virtue. For instance, almost as famous as the co-founder of Infosys, Narayana Murthy, is the myth and the occasional reality that he travels economy. But this chair, which I like very much by the way and consider the most meaningful thing money can buy, reveals the impossibility of economic and social equality, and the simple hypocrisies of many activists who claim to fight for just that.

The former finance minister of Greece, Yanis Varoufakis, who is a Marxist and also appears to know what that means, and is an economist who has become hugely popular by deploring the rich, said in a lecture that he delivered in 2013:


    My personal nadir came at an airport. Some moneyed outfit had invited me to give a keynote speech on the European crisis and had forked out the ludicrous sum necessary to buy me a first-class ticket. On my way back home, tired and with several flights under my belt, I was making my way past the long queue of economy passengers, to get to my gate. Suddenly I noticed, with horror, how easy it was for my mind to be infected with the sense that I was entitled to bypass the hoi polloi. I realised how readily I could forget that which my leftwing mind had always known: that nothing succeeds in reproducing itself better than a false sense of entitlement. Forging alliances with reactionary forces, as I think we should do to stabilise Europe today, brings us up against the risk of becoming co-opted, of shedding our radicalism through the warm glow of having ‘arrived’ in the corridors of power.

Yet, knowing all this, Varoufakis did not perform a very simple task: exchange his ticket with any of the unfortunate passengers in the ‘long queue’. He had to just tell the airline that he wished to switch places with one of the unfortunate people he was entitled ‘to bypass’. It is unclear why he even accepted the expensive first-class ticket in the first place from ‘the moneyed outfit’. Surely, he did not need to go to the airport to know that a first-class ticket had been issued to him, and that a first-class ticket, which is usually priced five times the economy fare, is the very opposite of equality. Surely, this was not the first time he was travelling first class.

Articulation of the conscience is a beautiful invention of the intellectual class. This expression is not chicanery; there is something deeply honest about it. Yet its primary function is to let words and confessions and good thoughts compensate for hard sacrifice.

As we saw in one of the previous chapters, vulgarity is complex, it is not all bad. In a world where people aspire to money and not class, and there is a perception that money is more gettable than refinement, some elements of vulgarity, like mansions and luxury sedans, are appealing. But there is also a danger—from bitter, radical, or just unhinged individuals. That is the reason why some traditional communities took to austerity. One of the problems the Malayali Billionaire faces when he tries to hire CEOs or other top executives is his company policy that no one can travel business, even on long international flights. He makes an exception for himself on long flights—that is, if he gets a good deal from his travel agency, which he incidentally also owns. He takes circuitous routes and long layovers to save money. When he goes to the First World, a senior executive told me, one of the parameters he uses to choose a cafe is that it has free Wi-Fi.

ONE of his top executives, whose mandate it is to encourage the poor to buy gold from his company, and not just pawn their gold with it, is a Marwadi. He says there are unsettling similarities between the Christian Malayalis of central Kerala and Marwadis, who are Jains who settled in some districts of Rajasthan and Haryana. ‘It’s as though a mother had a pair of twins and they got lost and one went to a region in Rajasthan and another went to a region in Kerala, and the twins started two different lines of descendants.’ The filmy analogy ends here and does not portray one of the twins as evil and the other as the virtuous. The most striking similarity between the Marwadis and the Christians of central Kerala is the seemingly congenital need for financial prudence that borders on miserliness, and reverence for social order and austerity. There is one difference though—the Marwadis do splurge on their weddings. Christian Malayali weddings are much simpler affairs, especially if you know the wealth of the groom and the bride.

Did the austerity of moneylenders transform the average Indian’s contempt for debt and lending?

Our Malayali Billionaire does own tastefully furnished homes and comfortable cars and goes on foreign vacations, but these are unremarkable luxuries that even an upper-middle-class Indian enjoys today. His sister-in-law says, ‘We are those Indians who will find the sealed water bottles on the cruise too expensive. You will find us in the queue to the tap to fill water bottles.’

Austerity is not abstract. It is not even subjective. Nor is austerity a definite absence of affluence. Austerity is possible only when one is able to afford everything it rejects. And what it rejects is most of the useless products that capitalism creates for the super- rich. Ratan Tata, for instance, was austere compared to most Indian billionaires. Mahatma Gandhi took austerity to such lengths that he dressed in a way that was an exaggeration of the common man even in his time.

But why are the austere austere? What advantage did the austere have, historically? Does austerity contribute to the morality of their profession? Are people in unpopular professions more likely to project austerity? Historically, across the world and especially in India, did the austerity of lending communities, like the Jews, the Marwadis of Rajasthan, and the Syrian Christians of Kerala, save them from revolutions? Also, how is it that whole communities demonstrate this puzzling human behaviour—to deny themselves what their money can easily buy?

Andrew Carnegie, one of the richest men who ever lived, said in his famous capitalist pamphlet, ‘The Gospel of Wealth’, ‘This, then, is held to be the duty of the man of Wealth: First, to set an example of modest, unostentatious living, shunning display or extravagance.’

This should make considerable sense to the rich, especially the rich in a poor country. There is something honest about extravagance as an expression, but it creates considerable ill will. The common public opinion in India that Vijay Mallya should be extradited by the United Kingdom to face charges here for defaulting on loans is largely a contribution of his extravagance. He is, in actuality, a more substantial man than what he portrayed, and people did enjoy him for his flashiness. The image is a problem, though, when good times turn bad. People eventually found him repulsive for living in luxury when he could not pay his staff their full dues.

Mallya has become the mascot of an opinion that at the highest levels of third-world capitalism, debt is a big immoral scam. Our Malayali Billionaire is the anti-matter of the Mallyas. He tries very hard to show he is frugal.

The austere are not playing a game. They want to be this way, they cannot be any other way. Culturally, religiously, psychologically they are compelled not to be extravagant. Very simply, they believe in austerity. And through this belief they inadvertently make their wealth a bit more palatable for the poor.



CHAPTER 14: THE NEWLY RICH VILLAGE
----------------------------------

THE first time I visited Gurgaon, then regarded a distant suburb of Delhi, was in 2007 when I went to meet the ‘sudden millionaires’, the middle-class and poor farmers who had suddenly become rich when they sold their land to developers. Gurgaon, by then, was already a burgeoning stretch of real estate with glass buildings, malls, and gigantic hives of apartments named after European things the courier boys could never pronounce. Piers were becoming bridges. Its marketplaces and restaurants teemed with EMI couples and their we- two-our-two children, and in the pale background, a dark melancholic Bihari maid. There was no evidence of any other time but the present, though Dronacharya was rumoured to have been born in a hamlet here. (‘Guru’ in Gurugram comes from him.) The hint that a different population once lived in Gurugram arrived sometimes in dramatic ways. A woman in Nirvana, a posh residential colony, recalled the appearance of a rustic woman at her doorstep. ‘I thought she wanted a job as a maid but then I realized she was looking for a house to buy.’

Descendants of the nomadic shepherds and farmers who had once settled in Gurgaon and grown bajra, jowar, and mustard in their arid lands, were, at the time, sudden beneficiaries of a spectacular revolution. The rise of the BPOs in the previous decade, the coming of other service industries, and the brute force of builder enterprise had converted almost worthless farmlands into properties that now fetched one to eight crore rupees per acre, a 500-fold increase over the last decade. Landed farmers who until recently had earned about ₹15,000 per acre in a year through farming, had become dollar millionaires. It was as if a whole district had won a lottery. And the carnival was only intensifying. In 2006, the year before my visit, sales of over 800 acres of land had been transacted. Big builders already owned 10,000 acres in Gurgaon, and they were buying more.

In the insanity of sudden affluence, farmers were buying cars, usually Škodas or Ford Endeavours for some reason, on cash down payments. Enchanted by the real estate industry, many had become land brokers. Some were lost to drinking and gambling. Women who used to wake up at four in the morning to finish household work, had now discovered bed tea. They even called it ‘bed tea’. When two neighbours crossed each other in the lane, there was a silent duel in their minds to decide who would say ‘Ram Ram’ first. ‘The man who is poorer should greet first,’ a farmer said of the new etiquette. Some men who used to be poor were not any more. The location of their land had turned out to be a lottery. They had suddenly become the new economic elite of their villages. Villagers who had once come together to conduct a marriage had now learned the art of grudging attendance and quick, sophisticated exits. They even knew what ‘sugar’ was. I heard a story, which I hope is not apocryphal because I want it to be true—of five brothers who walked into a school and asked the teachers if there was a smart girl of marriageable age among their students. The men said their family had earned three crore from a land sale but they were all illiterate. They had decided one of the brothers should marry an educated girl who would know how to handle the money.

Across India, the rising value of land is altering the behaviour of the former pastoral underclasses. Many have made money outright and become the new rich. For many, just the ownership of land has given a new swagger and dignity.

It is not as though they had never seen money spring from land before. By the time I visited Gurugram, the real estate revolution of the area had been about thirty years in the making, with the coming of the Maruti factory. Other industries had followed, but the change was swift and extraordinary now. Also, the very fact that the real estate boom was coming for some time created the new economic classes—because in the first boom, the old landowners had sold at what turned out to be modest prices. Now the value of land has risen hundreds of times.

I met Chatar Singh, one of the many fortunate farmers, an unexcitable tough fifty-six-year-old man with keen eyes. He was in farmer’s whites that had stains here and there. In a conservative estimate, he was worth over thirty-five crore rupees. He lived with two brothers, and together they owned five cars. Like many farmers in this area, he used the money he earned from selling his land to purchase more land further inland, where the land was still cheap. ‘When the land prices appreciate there, I will sell,’ he said, sitting on a cot and smoking a hookah. ‘This land where my house is, is about an acre and it is worth fifteen crore.’ His grandchildren went to English schools that cost over a lakh a year in fees. ‘Good schools,’ he said. ‘I went to a school where the teachers would come to the class and make sweaters.’

Chatar’s younger brother, Surinder, chuckles at the thought of men who had been rich when he had just been a poor village boy. ‘They had owned all the land. Thirty years ago, when they had thought there had been a real estate boom, they had sold most of their land for ten thousand rupees an acre. That land is now worth over a crore per acre. Today, their children drive taxis. That is life.’

The farmers who made money in land tried many businesses. The most successful investment for them, though, was investing in more land deep in the villages. It was hard and risky for outsiders to buy land, but the newly rich locals of the region could. And this made some of the savvy farmers even richer. Many are still holding out—that is why there are many cricket grounds in Gurugram. They have built a bank of land parcels, which they rent out to the cricket clubs of the new white-collared migrants.

The value of rural land, across India, has not only enriched the former poor but also made those who are yet to sell nominally rich even if their holdings are small. Many of them share the professions of the poor—like driving a taxi or working as security guards—but they have the swag of the landed and expect to be treated well.

The new-rich in their villages are culturally similar to them, as I mentioned in the chapter on the defeat of the asparagus-eating amateur Indian. This similarity makes them find common biases, common cultural foes.

In the beginning, when the highly educated sahibs first settled in Gurgaon as tenants, chiefly as corporate executives, some of them learned the hard way that it wasn’t wise to mess with the locals. One of my friends had an altercation with a tractor driver, who turned out to be a feudal lord of some sort. He landed up at my friend’s home with his friends and beat him up. This was new for the urban middle class hailing from other places, especially the south.

Today, Gurugram is a mature real estate market, but still in the tumult of growth. It has the reputation of being affluent. Some of its finest hotels are also called hospitals. There are apartments that cost over ₹100 crore; a penthouse went for ₹190 crore, and such buildings have their own spas. As a result, in 2023, many people were surprised, or maybe amazed, when mobs in Gurugram burnt a mosque and several vehicles.

THEY saw this as a contradiction—that shiny real estate for global companies and home to some of the highest paid Indians could also host religious riots. But then, in real life contradictions do not exist. That is the very nature of contradictions—they cannot exist. Like a paradox, they are solved when they are understood correctly. Gurugram is perfectly suitable for violence. The true character of a new town is never in the ‘new’ part; it is in the nature of its original population. Gurugram is less than 50 km from Nuh, Ground Zero for the spate of violence that spilled into Gurugram. Schools shut and people did not venture out. Muslim women fled, and those wearing vermilion began taking their place. A high proportion of the household help in the area, delivery boys, air-conditioner mechanics, and drivers are Muslim migrants. Many of them fled to Bihar, and it occurred to me that I now live in a place that some people would flee for Bihar.

Actual Gurugram, which is erroneously known as Old Gurgaon, emerged from a pastoral way of life and is even today rustic, and mindful of caste and religion. It has a history of communal tension, and is also one of the worst regions in India for women. Its mothership, Haryana, has a gender ratio of 879 females to every 1,000 males. (The national average is 940, and Kerala’s is 1,084.) Old-time Gurgaon is all around, even in the heart of the ‘new town’, not as adorable ruins but as omens. Dozens of free-range cows stand in the middle of the road, sensing a new mysterious privilege. The municipality is one of the worst managed in my experience. It is as though people who have been entrusted to bring the city to villages believe that they must bring villages to the city. There are hillocks of garbage that are burnt in the open, and many roads are broken. All this points to a political assurance that the votes come for reasons other than civic standards, and those reasons are economic and communal.

Gurugram and the villages around it are strongholds of men who claim to worship cows, some of whom assault unlucky Muslims who get caught with them. That aspect of the region has become more ruralized even as Gurugram continues to boom. The rent of affluent homes has almost doubled in the last two years. But the fundamental nature of the place is the nature of its original people; it is rural Haryana. Even Mumbai could not pretend for long that it was anything more than a Maharashtrian town.

One of the great civil wars of our times is the battle between the idea of the village and the idea of city; the battle between roots and rootlessness. It is a battle that the village is winning everywhere. In this victory are the signs of peace between the classes that are culturally similar.



CHAPTER 15: DESPITE INDIA
-------------------------



The grit and patience of the poor

Every four years, the world organizes a great festival to insult India. No other nation is more humiliated at the Summer Olympics than India. Indians perform poorly at the Winter Olympics too, but they are not as shameful because we are perceived by the world as a tropical nation. These days, India does win some medals at the summer edition but they are very few, especially when measured against a population of 1.4 billion. But India’s sporting mediocrity should not be what shames us. Instead, it is the fact that the Olympics is an accurate indicator of how terrible the Indian support system is, how poorly India cares for its own, and how difficult it is for talented people to survive in India even in objective fields like sports, which are controlled by some of the worst human beings nature has ever produced—low, and mid-rung politicians. In the past, Indian Olympians have not only faced poor infrastructure, some of them have also accused officials of sexual harassment and other forms of torment.

You ask any parent of any social class whose child has some kind of sporting talent in any of the disciplines, and you will hear stories of how poorly and cruelly competitions are organized, how filthy and unsafe the facilities are. Children are made to wait for hours in the sun because officials, who have a strange desire to dye their hair jet black, arrive late. Athletics events are held in dangerously humid conditions. There are under-15 swimming events in Gurugram in outdoor pools when temperatures soar to 45° Celsius. It is from these formative rungs that the future Olympians come, and they are designed to kill the spirit of young athletes, or just kill them.

That is why the very existence of Indian athletes of global calibre is not because of India, but in spite of India. The shirts of Indian athletes at the Olympics should not say ‘India’ but ‘Despite India’. And the most thrilling aspect of Indian athletes who triumph at the Olympics is that they are so exceptional that despite everything India threw at them, they have reached so far.

The middle class opts out of this sporting torment and instead becomes a consumer of national pride through the efforts of the poorer people. Like they employ drivers and gardeners, they also employ the poor to overcome the realities of India and somehow triumph against other nations.

WHEN Jaideep Sahni, the screenwriter for Chak De! India, began shadowing the women’s field hockey team, he was not sure why he was doing it. He thought he might make a documentary film about them, or write a book. He found the conditions in which they trained appalling. They were impoverished and undernourished. Almonds and other wholesome snacks, which in theory were to be provided to them, never reached them. ‘Most of them were thinner than the thighs of white players,’ he told me. Their bathrooms were dark because the electric bulbs had been stolen. When they had to change their clothes they stood behind a jersey held by two teammates. Then came the tracksuit problem. Their tracksuits were two years old and they did not know how to procure new ones.

The team’s coach, an Olympic gold medallist in field hockey, approached several people for help but was turned down. Then he decided to meet a politician in Bengaluru, where the women’s field hockey team was training at the time. According to Sahni, who accompanied him, the politician had a relative who ran a garment company. The coach explained the situation to the man and asked him to donate tracksuits to the team, worth about ₹200,000, or $4,000. The politician agreed on the condition that the company’s brand name be featured prominently on the apparel.

But the brand, though Indian, used the word ‘American’ in order to impress its buyers. The coach pleaded that it would be ridiculous for the Indian national women’s hockey team to wear tracksuits with the word ‘American’ on them. The politician asked him to look somewhere else.

Sahni found this baffling, but he himself was too broke to help at the time, struggling just to pay his mobile phone bills. He decided to go to Mumbai and ask some companies for sponsorship, but he failed to persuade any of them to part with the ₹200,000 for the women’s field hockey team. Infuriated, he swore to himself that he would make the team famous. ‘Even if that meant I’d have to write a mainstream commercial film in which the women’s hockey team danced.’ So he wrote the screenplay for Chak De! India which was rage masquerading as entertainment. The breakthrough for the project came when Shah Rukh Khan, who has played field hockey, decided to take a risk and star in the film.

When some multiplexes saw the trailers, in which women were shown playing field hockey, they refused to run them, saying they would only drive potential viewers away. But the film quickly became a hit. The women’s field hockey team watched the film laughing and whistling and crying.

Indian athletes who are not cricket stars usually have to endure humiliation and poverty. Olympians die in penury and in want of medical attention. Some have had to sell their gold medals.

Why don’t they ever rise in rage and burn everything to the ground? They just don’t. Despite India. But one Olympian did become a bandit.

Paan Singh Tomar held the national record in steeplechase, one of the toughest and least glorious track events. He represented India at the 1958 Asian Games in Tokyo, where he stopped midway in the race to throw away his new and unfamiliar spike shoes and then ran barefoot. He was also a soldier. When he went broke after his sporting career, he did not fade away meekly. He went after India. He became a bandit and roamed the ravines of central

India. Eventually, he was killed by the police in 1981.

A gripping Hindi film about him portrays him as a fierce athlete with a natural talent for middle- distance running, a patriotic soldier, and a young man in love with his wife. But everything changes after a land dispute with a powerful armed faction and his relatives.

According to the film, he goes to the police for protection, but an officer insults him, asks him what steeplechase is, and flings away his medal. Paan Singh’s family is attacked. He becomes a bandit to seek revenge, and later takes to kidnapping for ransom and killing police informants. He becomes yet another legend in the lawless ravines.

There were many like Paan Singh in the 1970s and 1980s (and even today) who took up arms because they had no faith in the government. Like many bandits of the time, he called himself a ‘rebel’. The real bandits, he says in the film, were in the Indian Parliament. (The audience clapped because they feel that way too.)

But most of India’s wronged don’t do that, the very subject of this book. They somehow survive, despite India; and they forgive, despite India. Across this nation, hundreds of millions of people use sheer grit to overcome everything India throws at them in their quest to make something of their lives.

Once, I saw a band of poor men create a film industry.

MALEGAON is a vast enchanting land with bright green rivulets and undulating pastures where lovers romp as food keeps falling from the sky. But this is from a pig’s point of view. For the men and women in this place, it is a failed miserable town built around power looms set up by ancient Muslims who had fled from the British after the sepoy mutiny of 1857. Some of its weavers and welders began to find immense joy when they began working on spoofs of Hindi films, all set in Malegaon. In 2003, when I worked as a staff writer for Outlook magazine, photographer Abhijit Bhatlekar told me about this place. Our story brought Malegaon national attention but it did not seriously change the plight of its early creators.

The actors of Malegaon are usually paid nothing, or almost nothing. They come for the free food on the sets, the excitement of being part of a movie, the minor fame that will come their way if a film is completed and released, and the hope that someone in Mumbai’s film industry might spot their talent.

A Malegaon film costs a few lakhs of rupees. The producers are usually small-time businessmen who often go broke or stop the funding when they feel a director is spending too much too soon. The films are made with camcorders. To achieve some ambitious shots, a cameraman is sometimes placed at one end of an ox cart and onlookers are asked to push the other end down so that the camera is slowly hoisted in the air. As the Malegaon moviemakers clearly cannot afford dollies, bicycles are used for tracking shots.

By the time I discovered the film industry of Malegaon, its artists had already spoofed some of the greatest Hindi film classics, including Sholay. At the time of my visit, they were shooting Malegaon ki Lagaan.

Farogh Jafri, the director of Malegaon ki Lagaan, was wearing a torn T-shirt. He ran a small tile- polishing business, but evidently, he did not earn enough from it. He was a distraught man when I met him. He had secured ₹30,000 as funding for the film from an employee of the local electricity board on the condition that the man’s son would be cast in the film and that shooting would be completed in fifteen days. But for a number of reasons, the shoot had stretched to over a year.

The cinematographer, who also owned one of the very few camcorders in Malegaon at the time, was in fact a wedding videographer who would vanish from the set or not turn up when he found a client. Also, sometimes the main actors would disappear. ‘But everybody would be here on the day the girls come,’ the producer said with bitterness.

Because Malegaon is a conservative place, actresses are usually outsourced—from the bottom rungs of Mumbai’s desperate starlets. A crew member on Malegaon ki Lagaan told me that these girls were ‘treated respectfully’ even offered expensive mineral water to drink, and ‘sent back with honour’.

When I last spoke to Jafri, about fifteen years ago, he was preparing to direct James Bond 007.5 of Malegaon. He had cast himself as Agent 007.5. His director’s fee was about ₹20,000, one-fifth of the total budget of the film. It will not make him rich, but life could have been worse. Around 2006, Malegaon’s film industry received a shock when the municipality shut down all the fourteen small theatres because they were caught screening pornography. But the film fraternity of Malegaon successfully begged the administration to reopen them.

Jafri mumbled some figures to me and said that if the Bond parody is a hit, it can actually make money for the producer. ‘A hit film may even run for more than ninety days, my friend. Because in our Malegaon, there is no garden, there are no public spaces where people can just go and have a good time. There is nothing to do. So they will go and watch my James Bond.’

In 2009, Malegaon released Malegaon ka Superrman. A few weeks before the release, Shafique Shaikh, who played ‘Superman’, died. He was a thin, delicate man with sunken cheeks and a frail chest. He was a power loom worker who earned about ₹400 a week. For the film, he wore his own baggy Superman costume with long red socks and rubber slippers. In the film, he plays a superhero who is accident-prone, who flies over a train engine and is very surprised when he is engulfed by a burst of smoke, who is spat upon by people in passing vehicles when he flies low over Malegaon, and who is reluctant to save a drowning person because he is afraid of water.

Superman’s archenemy is a tobacco tycoon. Like all Malegaon films, Malegaon ka Superrman also conveys social, civic, and moral lessons.

The film tells young people that they should not consume tobacco products and that they should not spit on the road.

Shaikh himself was addicted to chewing tobacco, and he died of cancer. He was twenty-five. He left behind a wife and two daughters—one not yet three and the other just a few months old. The film was so important to him that his last wish was to attend its premiere.

The director of the film, Shaikh Nasir, told me that a show was arranged for Shaikh in a packed hall and that he watched from a stretcher. He enjoyed the laughter of the crowds and wallowed in the pride and vanity of an artist. Six hours later, he was dead.



Why Indians should tip more

There is a flaw in the way we demonstrate the grit of underdogs, how they survive the atrocious biases of race, class, and gender, or how they survive India. The flaw is present in the central thesis of this chapter. We extol the virtues of extraordinary people who have achieved extraordinary things through sheer grit. But most gritty people may have nothing to show for all their grit. I believe that just making a go of it in a hostile nation like India as an ordinary person is great in itself. That is why lucky Indians should be melodramatically kind to the unlucky. In this, excesso is better. Even in this there could be self-interest at play. Visible acts of kindness of a society are underrated in keeping it safe.

One summer in Madras, when I was eighteen, I sold Funskool Toys door to door. My assignment was to walk down the roads of a fancy area of the city, in the searing sun, carrying a massive bag, and ring doorbells. Somewhat new to poverty, I prepared myself for humiliation. But I was stunned by the world of strangers and the persistence of kindness even though I was not a good salesman of board games and I did not sell a lot.

People drew me into their homes, offered water, even juices. Girls made eye contact. Housewives in nighties, with towels over their breasts, spoke so kindly the boy misunderstood. Old men spoke about education and the future of the nation. One man challenged me to a chess match on the terrace; if he lost he would buy my boardgames.

The experience deprived me of a strand of profitable bitterness. The world was not a cruel place. If you don’t include those pieces of shit—Pomeranians. We have an opportunity today to convey such a myth or reality to millions of young men and women. Through kindness, yes, but tips chiefly. Not only in restaurants but in other places, like our own homes. With whole warehouses of goods now coming home in the arms of the poor, there is much we can do with money that would otherwise stay in the desk drawer. We can push billions of rupees into the economic system even as we convey to the bitter men and women who are not like us the following truth: we are not so bad; you don’t have to count on crooked politicians alone.

Indians in the capitalist system who earn more and more through diligence or charlatan ways, and pay more and more direct taxes, indirect taxes, and cesses, are greater philanthropists than the thirty-year- old communist student leaders whose hearts bleed for the poor. Even so, there are moral reasons why we should do more.

Sadly in actuality, and my experiences as an impoverished teenager notwithstanding, forget tipping, many Indian consumers are plain villainous. There was a time when Amazon and Flipkart stopped delivering to Noida, a dismal place near Delhi. According to an Amazon employee, who explained the action, ‘Our delivery boys were locked up in bathrooms, some beaten up for the most silly reasons ever—‘give me my change of ₹2, the POS (point-of-sale) not working, the order was late. ’ Also, people in Noida, and other places, too, exhibited the tendency to order an iPhone, claim it was a Nokia, and return a piece of brick or biscuit packets in the box instead of the phone. And, several people ordered cash on delivery just for fun. For reasons such as these, it will be hard to convert India into a tipping nation, but the effort would be worth it and it would be an exquisite political act.

Any form of economic behaviour is usually not rational, it is merely a habit. That is the reason why in many societies, people tip only waiters but not other professionals who perform as much or far greater service. Like shop assistants. Actually, as Mr Pink pointed out in Reservoir Dogs, even McDonald’s employees are not tipped. ‘. you don’t see anyone tip them, do ya? Why not? They’re serving you food. But no, society says don’t tip these guys over here, but tip these guys over here.’

Even in other types of restaurants, where the idea of tipping is deeply set, Indians give grudgingly and poorly when a service charge is not included in the bill. If Indians start tipping as though it is a religious duty, it would not only infuse a reasonable amount of idle cash into the economic system but may also contribute to the goodwill between the republic of the middle class and that of the poor. A truly courageous reformer (which eliminates most politicians) would coax Indians to tip more instead of giving money to the God who saves in fixed deposits.

Tipping also includes asking taxi drivers to keep the change, and give even more. And letting the unsung inventor of surge pricing, the Indian auto driver, overcharge you within reasonable limits.



CHAPTER 16: A THEORY ABOUT HOW GOOD AND BAD THINGS HAPPEN
---------------------------------------------------------

SAM Pitroda was one of the most famous men in India in the 1980s. As the seer of India’s telecom, he was responsible for billions of long-distance calls of Indians getting through. With that French beard and words that a man with a French beard is expected to say, he looked like a man who would never win an election in India. Yet, he was one of the most powerful Indians because he was a beloved of the Gandhi family. A few days before the 2024 elections, he said that India should enforce inheritance tax and that the wealth of the rich should be redistributed. It was a very 80s moment from a superstar of the 80s. The BJP rejoiced and pounced on it. It successfully framed his statement as a Congress manifesto. That’s odd.

Why was the BJP joyous as though Pitroda had blundered? Wouldn’t India’s poor love what he said because, after all, what he said was that when the rich die, some of their money should be taken away from their spoilt kin and given to the poor. Yet, the BJP amplified his message as though this was a nation filled with billionaires; the Congress disowned Pitroda’s statement, saying he was on his own. Many young men and women who appeared to be from modest backgrounds went to a Congress office to protest against inheritance tax, and ‘wealth redistribution’.

It looked like nobody fully understood what they were doing. That fact does not concern us in this chapter.

What is useful about the controversy is that it gives us a rare peek into how humane ideas come to be. There is something about the way Pitroda said it, and the fact that he even said it, that tells us how ideas like inheritance tax may have come about in many countries. (India, too, had it, but it was abolished by the Rajiv Gandhi government in 1985, around the time when Pitroda was at the prime of his influence.) How does a society come up with an idea like inheritance tax when it is against the rich, which includes politicians and other powerful people? How does society bring in reforms when powerful people stand to lose from change? Why do good things happen in India?

You can say that change happens because some people are very lovely and they bring about good things. You can say that Sam Pitroda wants an inheritance tax because he is an exquisite person, he is better than most of us, and he wants change so that millions of poor people will be enriched. This could be true, but I don’t believe that. Nor do you.

If reform is not caused by the uncontainable good of good people, then why does it happen? What is actually going on? The reformation of society can be explained without giving too much credit to reformers. Society changes when the second rung of social power takes on the most powerful. Usually, this second rung is the old elite. When they become the underclass in a new world, they try to restore the balance of power through a moral cause, chiefly in the name of the poor. It works.

Much of what I believe in this regard is contained in this speech of a character in my novel, Miss Laila, Armed and Dangerous:


    Activism is always a feudal system in which nobodies are in the care of somebodies. It begins in a special moment when the elite of a system becomes the underclass in another system.


    …women, for instance, in a system controlled by men. Affluent blacks in a system of whites. High-caste Indians in America, rich third-world Muslims in the West. Writers in Indian languages in a nation where English language writers are culturally dominant. They are all elites of one system who become the underclass in another system. There are more. The progeny of old money, in an age where crass new money has overtaken them and made real estate too expensive for them. Mere millionaires in a system of multi-millionaires. Mere multi-millionaires in a system of billionaires; Billionaires, too, in a system of highly intelligent machines whom they defame because they have no one else to fear. Gandhi, the affluent upper-class racist in a system of whites who once denied him entry into a First-Class train compartment. Ambedkar, an elite low-caste, in a system of Brahmins, who too was denied entry into a First-Class compartment. It appears that if only passenger trains were equitable systems, many revolutions would not have occurred.

THE other reason why society changes is that children do not want to be their parents. Sometimes this includes children of rich parents having exaggerated contempt for some qualities of their parents, and choosing something their parents would never do, especially virtuous things. But mostly, change occurs because of the first reason, because the second rung, or the aristocratic class is sick of the monarchs. What can hurt the modern monarch more than the society taking away half of what they planned to bequeath to their genetic material?

Pitroda was a part of the elite of a time when class and sophistication alone could take you very far up the social peaks. In a changing world, very different people now occupy the peaks that once belonged to him or his friends or his sponsors. The capitalists themselves look very different now from his time.

What would you do if you were him and you wanted to mess with the new crude elite? Maybe you would suggest that their children should not simply inherit their fortune freely. They should pay a price for their luck. As this book likes to repeat, so often activism is the war of millionaires against billionaires. Across the ages, all of activism is just this—the second rung of a society fighting against the top rung in the name of a public cause.

A consequence of such a war is that aristocrats have to share some power with the masses. This is in the origin of that celebrated document Magna Carta from where, in the world according to the whites, democracy emerges. To fix the king, the barons had to say great things, modern things. That the king will not punish a person until his guilt is proven, or that the state will not usurp the land of its people, and that in peacetime England would protect the merchants as they moved about in the region.

A year after he was forced to accept the Magna Carta, King John cancelled it. In the centuries that followed, the document was forgotten for vast periods of time, and resurrected sometimes. Eventually, the British merchant class began to invoke it every time they had to ask monarchs to back off. By the seventeenth century, the idea of equality and fairness encoded in the Magna Carta helped landowning feudal lords to irreversibly diminish the monarchy in England. The price that British aristocrats and merchant class had to pay for this victory was democracy.

We have been trained to think that in a revolution, ordinary people come out of their homes and overthrow a ruler. Yet, I cannot think of a revolution where this was the actual case. Even the French Revolution was not that simple. As I have noted previously, in every revolution, the second rung of the elite has used the broad idea of equality and fairness to trounce their oppressor. This is true even of the Indian freedom movement. Most of the icons of the Indian freedom movement were from the highest caste, but still a social rung below the British. We can frame the freedom movement as the aristocrat’s use of morality to mobilize the masses and defeat his oppressor.

We see the phenomenon at play today in all forms of activism. It is always moral, always for a better world, always a war of one rung of the elite against their oppressor, but fought through recruits. The aristocratic handlers of revolutions are often not charlatans. They may believe that they are not acting in self-interest, but out of intelligent compassion. That is why they are so effective.

IN academic anthropology (by which I mean anthropology that is not art), there is something called Symmetric Schismogenesis. In this phenomenon, people assume the opposite views of their rivals and then take these views to the extremes simply to differ from the other camp.

Most people do not have strong beliefs. They arrive at them by developing the exact opposite views of those they despise. This is the reason why intellectuals find it hard to transmit wisdom. The problem is never that people are too dumb to see wisdom. As I have argued elsewhere in the book, people dislike wisdom if they dislike the mouth it comes from.

The political drama in the United States that Donald Trump’s second term unleashed gives us a chance to see this clearly.

The legend of Elon Musk was originally the creation of wokes, who include people who call other people woke. Wokes are tremendous in building cult figures very fast because they talk a lot about what they adore with others who adore the same things. At the time, the US tech industry was not called ‘techbros’; I think it was called Silicon Valley, if my memory is correct, and tech billionaires were all ‘good guys’. Facebook was good, cryptos were good, and Apple was so good that the sort of people who would have been hippies in the previous generation stood in midnight queues every time the corporation released a new product. These cool coders were going to change the world. And Musk was this environmental warrior because he had acquired an electric car company called Tesla and was out to kill the internal combustion engine. Musk said things that wokes loved. He spoke about climate action and direct democracy, for example. He sounded like a Democrat. He worked with Barack Obama and donated to the Democratic Party, too. So what happened?

It does happen routinely—men who appear to have the same politics as women are soon revealed as charlatans, or just misunderstood. Still, how did he get associated with Caucasian racism? Does an adult really change his political convictions so fast?

Donald Trump was a registered Democrat for nearly ten years; he donated to the party. For decades, as an American celebrity, he was probably a regular New York liberal who did not take himself seriously. What could have happened to him? Why is he at war with the whole empathy industry? How has a man not known for believing in anything suddenly become the world’s most impactful conservative ideologue? It is in fact odd to describe him with any serious word that would end in ‘gue’.

The political direction that Trump and Musk took was influenced by those who vilified them. They are now at war with all that was dear to their deriders: welfare state, bureaucracy, unelected institutions, liberal media, wokedom, migrants, diversity, feminism, transgender rights, and probably all of the humanities. Trump and Musk were given a political direction by the type of people they cannot stand.

This is how it works with ordinary people too. They are not public figures, so they may not have public enemies. The people they despise are their preachy colleagues, nasty bosses, acquaintances. And spouses, who are the most underrated counter- influencers. Many political ideas of ordinary men and women are shaped by their dislike for their spouses. All mainstream ideas have a point. They have to be moral, otherwise they would not have survived infancy to go mainstream. Morality is the tax an idea has to pay to survive.

Thus, the idea that reform occurs because some people who are better than us transform society is one of our most stupid. Generally, activists are pawns in a complex game between the elites, or pawns of their own ordinary evil that they do not fully realize. This is why I say, the young who are of sound mind, and this is an important classification, should quit full-time activism, refuse to work in non-profit organizations, sack themselves as humanitarians, and be suspicious of the hyper-morality that emanates from the West. They should instead make money, or do well in the material world in other ways, or at least start a doomed business. They are more useful to society this way.

I do not say for a moment that the young should not be interested in the welfare of other people, or that they should quietly tolerate despots and become pliable cogs in the wheel of capitalism. In fact, I agree with the first principle of our society, which I see this way: ‘If we are lucky, we must take care of the unlucky; if we are strong, we must take care of the weak; everything else is merely an argument about the best way.’ It is just that I feel the best way that the young, especially those who come from privilege, can serve their nation better is by opting for the for- profit material world, rather than choosing the easy option of festive grandstanding and do-gooding, which is often harmful, at best useless, or an inefficient way to make the world a better place.

There are good arguments against such a view. Material success is tough, a mere lottery, and often rigged. Why should the young be trapped in the unhappiness of material ambition; why should they waste their lives in dull corporate slavery? And, no amount of taxes deposited with the government or invisible jobs you create by expanding the economy can match the excitement and meaning of direct intervention in a social cause. Even so, my argument against early activism and hyper-morality among the youth rests on the idea that good happens, chiefly, when the second rung pulls down the top rung in self-interest. Everything else is the concealment of this. There is something activists whisper about their profession but cannot state in public. Humanitarianism has become a magnet for the mentally unstable who are compelled or wish to see unhappiness in everything. It is not a place where the happy can thrive. As there is a perpetual over-articulation of all that is bad in the world, it is a dangerous place to be in for the suicidal or merely the gloomy, like young people often are.

Barack Obama once exhorted the youth to create change by asking them to ‘make good trouble’, borrowing the phrase from an American civil rights activist. ‘Good trouble’ is not as naive as Obama’s imagination of ‘moderate Taliban’. The young can make ‘good trouble’. What is ‘toxic’, to deploy a useful modern term, is full-time idealism. The ‘joy of giving’ is a pleasure that radiates from an emotional feudal system in which some people feel a sense of importance over the miserable. And that joy of facile self-congratulation can be an unmatched intoxicant.

The arguments I have made against full-time activism notwithstanding, I must say that the occasional heartfelt do-gooding of people from ‘good homes’ is one of the most appreciated (and visible) aspects of the upper classes, and in that way is good PR for us all.

WHAT is the relevance of ‘good guys’ who always lose? What is the purpose of people who stand for harmony, equality, and freedom, but cannot draw the support of citizens who also want harmony, equality, and freedom? What is the point of an activist who speaks to the masses when the masses do the exact opposite of what he asks them to do? In a democracy, what is the meaning of public rejection?

Over the past decade, strongmen have trounced humanitarians. How should we view the people who have been fighting the strongmen? I am not referring to professional politicians alone. I am chiefly talking about the long tail of politics that is not called politics. Idealists, journalists who have not sold out, poets, developmental economists, humanities scholars, and writers with a moral compass.

THE fact that those who stand for the finest human virtues lose against strongmen does not mean that people have no respect for those virtues. Most humans hold the same set of virtues dear, but the hierarchy in which they are held at any point in time is what sets us apart. At the moment, for instance, most Indians and Americans and Europeans, okay most of the world, are at a stage where they wish for swift prosperity more than a sense of harmonious unity with the others. But this does not mean they are dismissive of unity or even the well-being of other people. So that is the relevance ‘good guys’ have. They are conservationists of a society’s best qualities; they ensure that just because some virtues are not popular any more, they do not slide further into oblivion. It is a voice that can emerge only from defeat.

But shouldn’t human virtues have better champions, people who know how to defeat the practical types? Not only do the ‘good guys’, especially in India, not know how to win, they also do not retire; they persist in their doomed fight because they have nothing else to do and they ensure that more talented activists with more potent fights in them do not rise.

To understand the true value of idealists, it is useful to take the example not of the worst and the mediocre among them, but the finest. So let us consider Ravish Kumar, a journalist and activist whose Hindi is exquisite and his calm son-of-the-soil lament as journalism is the very voice of India’s conscience. The arguments that Ravish makes do not win elections. And his overarching argument that the BJP is bad for the nation is something that people periodically reject. Yet, Ravish is extremely popular. A few times, when I have thought that a group of drivers under a tree are watching porn, it has turned out that they are only watching a clip of Ravish. I have heard Ravish’s voice in this manner from passers-by. Never have I heard anyone listen to the prime minister’s podcast, ‘Mann ki Baat’. Now, now, I know this is all anecdotal, but I believe intuitively that Ravish’s monologues are more popular than Modi’s. Without a doubt, Modi is many times more popular, and he would demonstrate so if ever there is a vote. But voting is not all that people do. There are some healthy things Indians do, and among them is probably listening to the voices of their nation’s conscience.

The charm of Ravish is in his state of defeat. He accepts that all resistance to the BJP is futile in the near future, and that nothing he says or reports will change that. In that way, his voice is the pathos of the underdog, and that is pure art.

Such voices of lament, as of Ravish, convey to the poor that their concerns matter, that society, despite everything, has ‘a moral compass’.



CHAPTER 17: REVENGE OF THE POOR
-------------------------------

WHAT I found most poignant during Covid was how desperately people wished to live. You won’t guess that from how they eat.

The Indian government is as absurd. The way it tried to keep us alive during the pandemic, for a moment there I did believe it doesn’t want me dead. But India and I go a long way. What India loves is the idea of rescue. Have you seen how India behaves when a guy needs a heart transplant—oh my God, one city comes to a standstill to let an ambulance zip through to the airport, then another city comes to a standstill to let an ambulance zip through to a hospital. But every day it kills thousands of us just on the roads.

As an Indian, my standards are so low that my idea of exciting urban development is a Nitin Gadkari tweet. I enjoy reading the plans of the minister for road transport and highways—the great roads he is getting built, how he would punish Indians who don’t follow the law (this part especially), how he plans to replace vehicular horns with the sound of Indian musical instruments, and how very soon road travel from Chennai to Bengaluru would take only two hours. I did try to point out to him on X (formerly Twitter) that right now Bengaluru to Bengaluru is two hours. Indian politicians, in general, don’t give us any hope when they speak of our urban future. They speak of nonsensical things like ‘smart cities’ and ‘twin cities’. The fact is, India’s politicians and people have worked together to make Indian cities among the most unliveable and ugliest places on Earth. It is as though the poor have voted their politicians to power to ensure India is not so enjoyable to the rich. It is not entirely untrue. The whole spectrum of India’s poor, which in this context includes the lower-middle classes, decide Indian politics and, to an extent, Indian politics is the revenge of the poor. They have consistently voted in second-rate people for short-term gains and to control rival castes and Muslims. In that way, the poor do kill. They may harm themselves more or as much as they harm the upper-middle class in creating a political class that has created a largely unenjoyable modern nation, but that does not alter the fact that something of this hell is a result of spite, the spite that the average Indian feels for those who are richer than him.

Across a vast landscape, India is an urban catastrophe. I believe this will not change for a long time. There is no hope. I can think of only two phenomena that convey an accurate representation of modern India. One is the Olympics and the other is any Indian town. The games expose how poorly run India is and how unkind we must be to each other to fare so poorly even against small nations. Our cities convey the same idea.

India’s poor quality of life emerges from its national character. The political stamina that Indians have for useless issues, if a fraction of that emotion were dedicated to our decrepit civic infrastructure, the lack of parks and beauty, our lives would be instantly richer. But that is not going to happen. If clean air, order, cleanliness, and aesthetics are not ‘electoral issues’, then why must a class of entrepreneurs called politicians even bother, especially when they would be punished by the voters for focusing on them?

THE middle class does lament, but not very deep in their subconscious they themselves suspect that traffic congestion and clean air are luxury issues.

In fact, India’s rich and half-rich may feel sheepish about freely expressing even their half gloom, their mild depressions, the way the affluent in other nations can. India’s threshold for what constitutes misery is very high. Even in this golden age of self-absorption, you feel it is somewhat indecent to lament your reasonless gloom without a deep sociological or medical reason. By Indian standards, melancholy is a trivial problem, ‘emptiness’ is even scoffed at, even though sorrow need not always have grand reasons, and it usually does not. When the Balcony People feel a deep growing sadness and void within, they are also forced to quickly add, to save themselves from ridicule, that yes they are lucky, they have everything, it must be their fault; if they cannot breathe, it must be their fault because they cannot claim there is a knee on their neck.

Indians have always struggled to understand melancholy that does not have an earth-shaking reason. In their pursuit of grand reasons for their sorrow, India’s affluent often find the wrong grand reasons—like marriage, parents, capitalism, and caste. The fact is, many actual reasons for melancholy might not have any gravitas. Like insect bites, gut bacteria, bad food, sloth, boredom, and getting influenced by mad men philosophers.



Escaping India is getting expensive

India’s urban mess may come from the fact that modern India was not governed long enough by the country’s cultural elite. There was a lot of democracy too soon. The elite can be despicable, but they always know how to live well. Every great city in the West has been a beneficiary of the vision of a social elite and the ease with which they could enforce codes of social behaviour.

The fundamentals of modern India, too, were created by its elite, but they did not prevail long enough to nurture its cities. Some of India’s early leaders did not think cities were important. Gandhi, for instance, wanted to bet big on villages. He was a famous evangelist of hygiene, but he was indifferent to urban planning. He was the sort of man who wanted Indian children to learn handicrafts even before their formal primary education (an idea that lurked in India’s first five-year plan).

In response to their failure to fix the country, the Indian upper class has always resorted to migration. Not just by leaving India. The rich flee India even within, by creating islands of comfort and safety that the other India encroaches upon only as maids, drivers, delivery boys, and GST. That is more or less what Gurugram’s residential real estate boom is about.

A good thing about the winter smog in Gurugram is that you do not see most of Gurugram. It is an ugly, dismal city and home to some of the richest Indians. Now and then, in the winter, I see its most famous residential building looming up in the smog. People tell me (at the time of sending the book to press) that some flats there cost ₹100 crore each. For no fault of mine, a broker sent me a news clipping that said a 16,000-square-foot penthouse in the building sold for ₹190 crore.

That apartment building makes me chuckle, especially when I see it in the smog. I chuckle silently. I don’t think anyone chuckles aloud when they are alone. But I don’t know yet why it is funny, at least not very clearly. Maybe it is because the building reminds me that the experience of being super-rich in India is one of the most overpriced things in the world.

In the best areas of London or New York, you pay a premium for what the city offers, and what the city offers is available to everyone. In India, it is the opposite. The city is the problem, and you pay to shut it out, along with most Indians of your ilk.

Some people tell me that not all the people who have bought flats in that residential tower actually live there. But many do, and among those who don’t, most still live in India, in similar places. In any case, no one disputes that most of India’s wealthiest people do live in India, even though a few may have bought Western citizenships. Some people tell me that the kind of people who live in that building in the smog have many homes across the world. But this misunderstanding of the rich comes from those who are not as rich. The human perception of home is the same across people of varying wealth levels.

People may own many houses, but they usually have one home. A home, by definition, need not be a single unit, but it usually is. It is made up of people, love, and compulsions. And people, love, and compulsions are not as portable as people think, even for the rich. A home is where you are stranded. Only a home can have good reasons to keep you there. It can even keep you in a dismal grey town from where you escape now and then, but only to always return.

I have been inside that building. Its residents invited me once to speak there. They have their own plush theatre. They showed me their unmatched spa and heated pools. You don’t have to ever leave this building. It is a haven designed to escape India within India.

Good life in India is an archipelago of private islands. Even the upper-middle classes live like this— on islands.

When affluent Indians pay for a home or lifestyle, they do not pay for the value of the thing but for the promise that India cannot enter it, at least not easily. It’s not just homes, but also schools, resorts, restaurants, and even cinemas that are in reality clubs that keep the real India out.

The price for this is just about right for the asparagus-eaters, the upper-middle classes. For ₹5–10 crore, they can find a home in a beautiful Gurugram colony where most of India is stopped at the gates. Women can walk without worry and children find safe ground. Even this is slightly overpriced because it is comparable to the price of a home in, say, Brooklyn. But the super-rich have to pay inordinate amounts, not only to claim to be rich in India, but also to live and feel like rich people and exorcise the spirit of real India. They either build an entire skyscraper just for their family or live in resort-like apartment buildings, paying atrocious amounts merely to escape India. The irony, of course, is that even after paying so much, they still cannot entirely escape the nation. They still have to live in an urban cancer that keeps growing untamed. They get stuck in traffic more frequently than novelists who don’t have to leave home, and they breathe toxic air.

The upper-middle class does spend a lot of money on its cocoons but it has also developed ways of exclusion that cost nothing or very little. They use what is called ‘culture’ and a bit of sophistication to exclude most Indians from their daily lives. In the Madras of my childhood, for instance, it was classical music and dance. Today they use their Ivy League education and narrow specializations in luxury streams to nurture exclusive clubs where the other India simply has no chance to present itself. The super-rich, on the other hand, only have money. And money has long divorced class, so some old-money people scoff at the neighbours and even friends they now have to endure. The super-rich across the world face a general problem with capitalism, which does not serve its true masters well. There are no real products for them. They must shoot themselves to space or sink to ocean depths to see and feel something the rest of the world cannot. Overpriced bags, isolated giant-silo homes, and 100-metre-long yachts, too, might be just outlandish expressions of the fact that even though money can buy happiness, there is no kind of happiness in the market that only a lot of money buys. The best phone, cinema, or even holiday is available to the merely affluent.

But at least the billionaires of the West can dabble in longevity science or bet on politics without facing ruin if their bets turn bad. The super-rich of Gurugram only have a building in the smog.



CHAPTER 18: INEQUALITY CANNOT BE SOLVED
---------------------------------------

THERE is a sort of inequality that looks bad in statistics. For instance, when you look at the top 1 per cent and how much of a nation’s wealth they own. But there is another sort of inequality that is not so lamented but is more destructive. The billionaires don’t figure in this. It is the headstart that the upper-middle class has on the rest in all the turfs where they compete with those below them.

In almost everything they do, billionaires and their children are not in competition with the rest of society. They have their own charmed universe where they do not even properly mingle with people who do not come close to their wealth. But consider the asset millionaires: they compete unfairly with the rest of society in entrance exams, finance jobs, the start-up culture, in cinema, literature, and the arts. Their head start is not in their material assets alone, which is an overrated part of privilege. Their true unfair advantage is in social contacts and the doors of the clubs that they hold to control who enters. Seen this way, the billionaires are far less guilty of inequality than what the upper-middle class creates. The objective of any family, whatever be its resources, is to provide an unfair advantage to its children.

Can something as pervasive as inequality be solved? Or is inequality the most natural consequence of human nature? Actually, what the hell is equality? Is equality one of those thought experiments of European philosophers, like equilibrium, black body, and Marxism, which their acolytes have taken too seriously as an actual state that can be achieved? An amusing thing about inequality is that the people who talk the most about it are all beneficiaries of it. Is social ‘inequality’ like gender inequality, merely a complaint by the 98 percentile against the 99th percentile? Mere millionaires loathing multi-millionaires; multi-millionaires loathing billionaires.

Some argue that social inequality began with the invention of mass production; some argue that inequality is so fundamental to human nature that it did not have to wait for the rise of capitalism to permeate society. The master and the servant may have slept in the same room before the Industrial Revolution, but still there was the master and the servant, wasn’t there?

Even if inequality is a natural consequence of human advancement, it does not mean it is not evil. We do not have to consecrate all that is natural.

Even so, all things considered, the poor of Mumbai today have a higher standard of life than Mughal emperors. Andrew Carnegie, while accepting that inequality is a creation of capitalism, wrote in ‘The Gospel of Wealth’, ‘Much better this great irregularity than universal squalor.’

But this is not a correct perception of poverty. Poverty is relative. The very existence of the rich creates poverty. The poor in a rich region may come with some higher standards of life, like the ability to buy denim jeans and a smartphone. But, as I showed in an early chapter, it is worse to be poor in a rich region than to be so in a poor nation.

The mechanism of greed can increase the general quality of life but cannot fix inequality because the whole point of greed is inequality. Do-gooding is inefficient because it attracts exactly those people who are useless in solving anything; the modern history of India is living proof.

There is another way of looking at the issue. If you use money as a measure, there will never be equality because that is the nature of money. To that end, inequality is a false lament.

People do feel lucky about their gifts—their youth, health, family, love, beauty, strength, intellect, and how gorgeous their village is. They are not in the perpetual gloom of being in the bottom 50 per cent at all. Yet, because money is so easy to measure, they are trained to consider themselves lesser people.

The failure of the world is not in creating economic equality, which cannot exist in the real world in any case. The failure of the world is its inability to show people the truth—that money is just one of the things that matter. Intellectuals have tried, of course, through their happiness indices. Writers have tried through their moral stories. But they have failed.

People desperately want many experiences that they can see in the lives of the most visible section of the rich—not the billionaires but the upper-middle classes. And everything that people desperately want becomes a ‘basic necessity’ of life. Every time a new way of life makes living better, its denial to the majority becomes one of the new meanings of poverty. That is why, as long as there are the rich, there will be the poor.
